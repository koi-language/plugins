# Story Structure: the Five-Act Shape

Source: John Yorke, "Into the Woods." Structural backbone to check any script/storyboard/multi-shot video against. Use to design AND diagnose, never paint by numbers.

## The universal story shape

All stories share one structure. Three templates collapse into one:
- **Monster**: a monster threatens a community; one person kills it to restore happiness. "Monster" can be literal or figurative (fire, a corporation, a mother).
- **Brave-new-world**: a hero stumbles into a strange world, transfixed then it turns sinister.
- **Quest**: a community needs a distant elixir; one tribesman makes the perilous journey.

All: plunge a character into a strange world, quest to escape, vanquish literal/figurative "monsters." Goal is always safety/security/completion/home. Kids reproduce the shape spontaneously: it's how minds order experience, not genre.

### Premise vs story
- **Premise**: "Once upon a time, somewhere, something happened." Just a setup.
- **Story**: add "and because of that I'm going to do THIS." The "something" is a problem (sometimes disguised as opportunity); the story is the journey to solve it. Detective/hospital drama = storytelling with impurities removed (a crime/sickness, then the hunt for the cure).

### JOURNEY THERE; JOURNEY BACK (the expanded single structure)
1. "Home" is threatened.
2. Protagonist has a flaw/problem.
3. They journey to find the cure/key.
4. Exactly halfway, they find it.
5. On the way back they face the consequences of taking it.
6. They face literal/metaphorical death.
7. Reborn with the cure; "home" is saved.

Sometimes literal (Orpheus). In films ending at the outward journey's end (E.T., Godfather) the return is buried inside: midpoint = first embrace of the missing quality; second half = reckoning. Mantel: journey into the wood = psyche's birth→death→rebirth; "we are found by being lost."

## The essential building blocks

Every complete story (even a six-line one) contains all of these: protagonist, antagonist, inciting incident, desire, crisis, climax, resolution.

### Protagonist
Audience avatar; their jeopardy is ours.
- MUST have an active, tangible goal. No desire = passive = dead on screen. Sorkin: "Somebody's got to want something, something's got to be standing in their way."
- "Care" ≠ "like." Never soften to make nice; niceness kills characters. Rough edges/darkness attract (Satan; Hitler in Downfall). Drama asks "you too?"
- Empathy roots in wish-fulfilment, not manners/motive (maverick cops: we all feel surrounded by idiots). Chekhov: "Tell me what you want and I'll tell you what manner of man you are."

### Antagonist
The sum of all forces of antagonism (everything obstructing desire). External (Bond villains), internal (cowardice, low self-esteem), or both (Jaws).
- Hitchcock: "The more successful the villain, the more successful the picture." Story is only as strong as its antagonism.
- **Structural law: protagonist and antagonist are OPPOSITES.** The antagonist embodies exactly the quality missing in the protagonist's life. Joker: "You complete me."
- Thriller antagonists want to control/dominate; historically marked by deformity as outer sign of inner damage. Internal antagonists cripple the host the same way.
- Inciting incidents arouse the antagonist; forces snowball until they confront the protagonist at the climax.

### Desire (the want)
Embody in a tangible object/person (Juliet, the Ark, Rosebud). Abstract wants (happiness) are too nebulous. **MacGuffin** (Hitchcock): object barely matters, importance bestowed by pursuit. But desire must connect to something primal: success, revenge, love, survival, protection of family/home. TV: weekly goal changes but always physically embodies the mission to save/preserve/enhance the protagonists' world.

### Want vs need (the flaw)
3D characters have a conscious external WANT and an unconscious internal NEED, usually not the same (Wiesler wants to punish, needs empathy). Propp's "lack" = what the protagonist is missing.
- The need is to overcome the flaw. Flaw present from page one; want crystallizes only after the inciting incident.
- Characters shouldn't always get what they want, but should, if deserving, get what they need.
- Strongest archetype: ego-driven goal abandoned for something more nourishing (Rocky, Tootsie). Getting both want + need is possible (Star Wars) but less resonant.
- The opening disruption threatens perceived security; the "want" seeks that security again, often in the wrong place. The gap between where they look and where it lies IS the engine of structure.

### Inciting incident
Premise made event; the "What if...?" Two jobs: disrupts the world AND awakens a desire. Schlegel's "first determinations"; the subject of the trailer. Precise placement: see dedicated section.

### The journey
Change is the heart of the quest; so is choice: the protagonist must finally choose how to change (Ripley's real quest = prove herself a mother by adopting Newt). Films where the protagonist changes internally have far deeper purchase; the audience's worldview realigns with the hero's.

### Crisis (the worst point)
A kind of death: someone close dies, hero seems to die, or all hope dies. US TV: "worst case"; BBC: "worst point." Max jeopardy, the "Oh no!" / last-break cliffhanger.
- Crystallizes the final dilemma: what kind of person is the protagonist? (Luke: computer or Force.)
- The choice forces the hero to face their need/flaw. External antagonists embody the inner fear; conquering what lies without requires conquering the chasm within.
- Stench of death: kill the old self and live anew, or deny change and revert (also a death). The right road is the harder one.

### Climax (the obligatory act)
Final showdown where the hero engages their dramatic need and overcomes their flaw. Sarcey's "obligatory scene" (really an act). Because the antagonist embodies the flaw, defeating the external obstacle liberates the internal need. Inciting incident asks "What will happen?"; climax answers "This." Usually the biggest iconic sequence, last ~25 min, often on alien territory. Skipping/underplaying = setup denied payoff. (No Country kills its protagonist at the crisis only as part of a wider schematic plan.)

### Resolution (denouement)
From "denouer," to untie: knots undone, every setup paid off, no strand forgotten. Final judgement: rewards to those who beat their demons. Modern endings can be open (characters drift into oblivion, or fail to marry, Four Weddings). Archetypes can be twisted deliberately (Wire kills Omar via a stranger to say "codes don't operate here").

## 2D vs 3D stories
- **3D (character change)**: protagonist has a FLAW; the story arcs their character; they discover who they are and heal themself.
- **2D (Bond, Columbo, Morse)**: protagonist has a DEFICIENCY OF KNOWLEDGE, not a flaw. "White bread," a dimension removed so we re-enjoy them. Drama IS change, so what changes is their KNOWLEDGE, on the same five-act roadmap; solving the crime heals the world where the inner journey heals the self.
- Both can coexist in one beat: Godfather II, Michael learns of Fredo's betrayal (2D) and silently decides to kill him (3D).

## Dark inversions (tragedy)
Same principles reversed. The "flaw" is a quality society calls good (Macbeth's bravery/humility); it corrodes along the same archetypal path until egotistical desire consumes the character. Gilligan: "Mr Chips into Scarface."
- A flaw at the start produces its opposite at the end: good→bad. Dark journey = selfless→selfish (reverse of Rick).
- Permutations by feeding a different flaw or reward/punishment: punish growth = tragedy (Lear); reward the dark protagonist = indict a sick society (Taxi Driver); make the hero worse than the monster (Frankenstein); leave blame ambiguous; celebrate the collapse.
- Macbeth = master diagram: humility overcome by arrogance ("none of woman born"), modest hero→tyrant; being tragedy, he dies.

## Change is the engine
The Godfather restaurant murder is the iconic image of what all drama is built on: change, and the internal act of will to pass between states. Wanting something forces change to get it (Kaffee must grow up to face Jessup). Walter White: chemistry is "the study of change... growth, then decay, then transformation." Learning is not a cliché to mock: learning is HOW characters change, to a pattern (Roadmap of Change).

## Three-act structure
Ramsay: structure is "two or three little surprises followed by a bigger surprise."
- An **act** = a unit of action bound by a character's desire, with beginning/middle/end; its end spins the narrative in a new, unexpected direction = **peripeteia** (reversal).
- No fixed count (Cyclops = 1, Seinfeld = 2, Raiders = 7), but the modern archetype is **three: set-up, confrontation, resolution** (Syd Field), with turning points near the end of act 1 (inciting incident) and act 2 (crisis). Predates film.

### Why three: the dialectic
We order the world dialectically, imposing order on randomness. Mamet: "thesis, antithesis, synthesis; boy meets girl, boy loses girl, boy gets girl."
- **Act 1 = THESIS**: flawed character in a limited world; inciting incident drops them down the rabbit hole.
- **Act 2 = ANTITHESIS**: the alien world embodies everything they lack. They try to return home while learning the new world's lessons; at their lowest (crisis) they must choose: confront armed with what they've learned, or slink back.
- **Act 3 = SYNTHESIS**: biggest battle (climax), assimilation, reward (resolution). Old + new integrate to balance.

"Subversions of expectation" at act ends = peripeteia: the portal from thesis to antithesis. Cliffhangers, inciting incidents, crisis points are the same device (a turning point into a new world). Even writers who denounce the form obey it: the shape is compelled.

## Five-act structure
NOT a different system: it refines three by inserting two extra act breaks inside the middle. First and last acts stay identical.

```
 3-ACT   |  ACT 1  |          ACT 2 (confrontation)         |  ACT 3  |
 5-ACT   |  ACT 1  |  ACT 2  |    ACT 3     |   ACT 4   |  ACT 5  |
            same     new       midpoint       new          same
```
Mapping: 3-act Act1 = 5-act Act1; 3-act Act2 = 5-act Acts 2,3,4; 3-act Act3 = 5-act Act5. Five-act reading illuminates how the middle works.

### History (why it carries authority)
- **Horace** (Ars Poetica, 1C BC): "no play shorter or longer than five acts" to be revived. Codified **Terence**'s practice. Baldwin: every Terence play in five demarcated stages:

| Act | Content (Baldwin) |
|---|---|
| 1 | Preparatory info leading to the resolutions that occasion the struggle |
| 2 | Preliminary moves/countermoves before the main battle |
| 3 | Opposing forces make their chief assault and seem to win |
| 4 | Heroes counterattack; opposition really lost but heroes not yet officially won |
| 5 | They win |

- **Seneca**'s five-part plays (Renaissance rediscovery) fed Jonson and Shakespeare (fits Terentian pattern whether marked or not; candle-trimming imposed breaks; bladders/standing audiences/candle duration demanded them).
- **Scribe** (1791-1861) industrialized it as the "well-made play": 400+ works, each act ending in a turning point/reversal, topical, poetic-justice endings. Ibsen founded "a new school" using Scribe's structure + serious discussion instead of last-act unravelling. Lesson: craft ≠ enemy of authenticity.
- Three acts resurfaced in the 19C (theatre comfort, fewer intervals); cinema inherited it. Five-act shape gives control over the sagging middle. (Even a 2007 "Yummy Mummy Lit" parody reproduces the pattern unknowingly.)

### Freytag's Pyramid and the dramatic arc
Freytag (1863) codified the Elizabethan shape as five stages:
1. **Exposition**: personae, place, antecedents; the germ of conflict.
2. **Complications**: "tying the knots"; interests clash, momentum builds.
3. **Climax of the action**: conflict peaks; hero at the crossroads.
4. **Falling action**: reversals, consequences; tension via false hopes/fears.
5. **Catastrophe**: resolution via downfall or victory/transfiguration.

Placing "climax of the action" in the MIDDLE (act 3) is the first articulation of the **midpoint** ("outburst of deed from the soul of the hero"). **Booker** sketched the same five: call to arms, dream, frustration, nightmare, thrilling escape/resolution. Both fit Shakespeare (Macbeth: prophecy/decision → king → Macduff defects → Lady M mad/M abandoned [worst point] → battle/killed).

What Booker missed: in act 3 things don't go wrong immediately: action **peaks at the midpoint apex** before fortunes reverse. Plot the fortunes and the "dramatic arc" appears:

```
                    MIDPOINT (apex) = key knowledge / point of no return
                         /\
             rising     /  \   falling action, consequences pursue
  commitment  o------ /      \  ------o reward/new home
     o_______/                \______/
   inciting                   WORST POINT (crisis, death in the air)
   |  ACT1  |  ACT2  |    ACT3    |  ACT4  |   ACT5   |
```

    INCITING  COMMITMENT  MIDPOINT  WORST  CLIMAX  RESOLUTION

### The five acts and their functions

| Act | Booker | Function | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Call to arms | Call to action | Flawed protagonist in limited world; home threatened; act crisis = the inciting incident (invitation to the forest edge). Catalyses next act AND whole story. |
| 2 | Dream | Initial objective | Short-term solution rooted in the flaw. A **second inciting incident** forces "things can't stay the same." Act crisis = choice old vs new self. Enters the forest. |
| 3 | Frustration | MIDPOINT: key knowledge | Experimenting with the new world. At exact center: moment of truth. What they feared in the first half they now embrace. Two halves mirror around the midpoint; act 3 crisis forces confronting earlier actions. |
| 4 | Nightmare | Doubt / regression | Consequences pursue; commitment tested; abandonment considered. Act 4 crisis = crisis of the WHOLE story (worst point; death in the air). Question: revert and die, or change and live? Home of "monologuing" (motive explained before the final battle). |
| 5 | Thrilling escape | Climax + resolution | Act-5 sub-goal = original story goal. Hero returns with a truth the tribe may not want. Archetypal antagonist embodies the flaw → external + internal battle are one. Using midpoint knowledge tested in act 4, defeat the antagonist, overcome the flaw, become complete. |

Value of five over three: control of the middle, cure for the sagging act 2, regular gripping turning points.

### Per-act notes (Godfather through-line)
Illustrates the functions table: (1) Michael innocent until his father is shot → the act-1 crisis catalyses "how will he react?"; (2) protects his father, then a second inciting incident (father set up again) changes him, foiling the hit exhilarates him, enters the forest; (3) story midpoint = act-3 midpoint: embraces the feared thing (shoots the cop), tastes the reward (Sicily marriage), knowledge "that can never be lost" ("sought"→"find"), then Apollonia's murder forces the return/elixir choice; (4) act-4 crisis = story crisis (Don dies), death of the old self; (5) archetypal antagonist embodies the flaw (external = internal), midpoint knowledge tested through act 4 defeats all enemies, completeness (baptized in blood).

### Worked 3D example (Thelma & Louise), one line/act
1. Camping trip (Louise repressed, Thelma naive); attempted rape, Louise shoots the attacker. (INCITING INCIDENT)
2. Run for Mexico; a call reveals Thelma's husband's true colors → commits.
3. Enjoy the new world; motel sex = MIDPOINT (both overcome flaws first time); Thelma robs a store; police get their first lead.
4. Police close in, old rape revealed; toy with surrender, give away location. (CRISIS)
5. Blow up the tanker; cornered, hold hands, accelerate, fly (framed as ascension).
Twin arcs: Thelma learns self-determination, Louise to let go (equal and opposite, meeting in balance).

### Worked 2D example (Spooks S3 finale), knowledge arc
1. Fiona & Danny kidnapped. (Inciting) 2. Kidnappers' demand; Adam captured learning of it. (First inkling) 3. Halfway MI5 plants a bug, now they KNOW. (Midpoint) 4. Bomb sewn in Khatera's stomach, led to the PM. (Crisis) 5. Adam talks her down, saves wife + country. (Climax): nothing known → first inkling → identity → full horror → mastery.

### The midpoint (the most neglected beat)
Almost exactly halfway: something profoundly significant, massive jeopardy escalation, point of no return (Macbeth III.4: "I am in blood stepp'd in so far...").
- **Function**: protagonist first fully touches the "elixir" (flaw-curing quality; Campbell's treasure in the enemy's cave) but can't yet use it, a powerful drug without the skill; acquiring mastery is the whole second half. End of the outward journey, start of the return.
- **3D**: first embrace of the needed quality (Michael shoots Sollozzo/McCluskey; African Queen opposites kiss then deny it, but the die is cast and enemies now know them).
- **2D**: moment of truth, first grasp of the forces against them (Bond under the laser learns Goldfinger), or physically holds the quest object (the Ark). In Christie, often the murder itself.
- **Design rule**: risk/reward, gain something vital AND ramp the jeopardy.
- Examples: Titanic hits the iceberg · chestburster in Alien · E.T. phones home · Bourne learns the truth · Vincent's adrenalin into Mia's heart · Antony turns the mob (Julius Caesar center) · Silva captured (subject) / Lektor seized (object).
- Pre-film pull to the center: Tom Jones' one inn, the Marabar Caves, Odysseus reaching Ithaca halfway. Lodge: "Symmetry matters more to writers than readers consciously perceive."

Truth of the story lies at the midpoint; the protagonist's second-half task is to assimilate what it means.

## The Roadmap of Change (Yorke's central paradigm)
How an unconscious flaw is surfaced, acted on, overcome. In tragedy, run in reverse (goodness corrodes to the same design). In 2D, substitute knowledge for character.

| Act | Stage | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No knowledge | Flaw unconscious; lives in the limited world it built. |
| 1 | Growing knowledge | Inciting world intrudes; first glimpse outside. |
| 1 | Awakening | Act 1 crisis (inciting incident) makes it undeniable; desire born. |
| 2 | Doubt | New world frightening; short-term fix rooted in the old flaw. |
| 2 | Overcoming reluctance | Second inciting incident: things can't stay the same. |
| 2 | Acceptance | Commitment: enters the forest. |
| 3 | Experimenting with knowledge | Trying the new rules, half in the old self. |
| 3 | MIDPOINT: KEY KNOWLEDGE | Elixir touched first time; truth; point of no return; jeopardy escalates. |
| 3 | Experimenting post-knowledge | Living with the power without mastery; consequences begin. |
| 4 | Doubt | Cost becomes visible; pursuers close in. |
| 4 | Growing reluctance | Commitment tested; abandonment considered. |
| 4 | Regression | Worst point; reversion beckons; death in the air. |
| 5 | Reawakening | New call to action; chance to rededicate. |
| 5 | Re-acceptance | Commits and pursues relentlessly. |
| 5 | Total mastery | Final hardest choice; flaw overcome (or corrosion completed). |

Flaw-planting (E.T.): act 1's "Why don't you grow up?" IS the flaw; every act after overcomes it (letting his friend go).

## Vogler's Hero's Journey mapped onto five acts
Vogler's 12 stages (reduction of Campbell's monomyth) = the same five-act shape from the protagonist's POV; the "supreme ordeal" = the midpoint. Full stage-by-act mapping (and every other guru's translation, Campbell's monomyth included): structure-worked-examples.md §VII.

Feed a real flaw and it maps (Strictly Ballroom: substitute "intimacy" for "elixir"). The elixir the homeland needs = the element the protagonist needs to cure the flaw.

## Fractals: the shape repeats at every scale
Dramatic structure is fractal. Stories→acts→scenes→beats, each carrying the same shape.

```
 STORY  [ set-up ... confrontation ... resolution ]
 ACT      [ set-up . confrontation . resolution ]
 SCENE      [ set-up . confrontation . resolution ]
 BEAT         [ action -> reaction -> (unexpected reaction) ]
 Each unit has its own protagonist, antagonist, inciting incident,
 journey, crisis, climax (often a midpoint).
```
- Every act is a mini-story (Raiders act 1: Indy vs Belloq, inciting = find temple, journey = retrieve idol, crisis = Belloq surprises, climax = idol stolen, resolution = escapes, even a midpoint).
- Acts are bound by a **single dramatic desire** completed within, with a turning point spinning toward a new goal. The desire-unit test finds act breaks in unmarked scripts (Raiders yields 7 clean stages).
- **Position, not shape, names the unit**: tripartite unit at the start = inciting incident; middle = midpoint; end = climax. Simple cells merge into complex units.
- Below the act: scenes have set-up/conflict/crisis/climax/resolution + mini inciting turning points; beats are action/reaction pairs ending in an unexpected reaction. (Scene craft in the scene-writing reference; here only the SAME shape matters.)

### Question and answer (the master formula)
Inciting incident and crisis are directly related: **the crisis embodies the worst possible consequence of the inciting-incident decision.** Q: worst consequence of Macbeth killing the King? A: his allies march on him. Structure at every level drives the protagonist to face their darkest fear (commitment-phobe → losing the loved one). Binds each act internally too. **Master formula**: first half asks "What is the worst consequence of this decision?"; second half answers "This is."

## The inciting incident, precisely placed
First acts are tripartite, mimicking the whole:
1. **Set-up** (with a mini inciting incident): protagonist alerted to a world outside their own.
2. **Confrontation**: react, pursue a course that precipitates a crisis.
3. **Climax + resolution**: crisis forces a decision propelling them into a new universe.

**The inciting incident is the crisis point (second turning point) of act 1**: a subversion, cliffhanger, antithesis, presenting the choice to step into the woods (Star Wars: step-parents murdered; Some Like It Hot: Massacre witnessed).
- **What vs how**: act 1 crisis = the "what" ("What's the problem?"); act 1 climax = the "how" ("This is how I'll deal with it"). Apocalypse Now: Willard taking the cigarette is the climax-decision.
- **The Deferred Call** (Campbell's "refusal," through five acts): the journey often begins at act 1's end but the underlying CHANGE only at act 2's end (Raiders; Witness: Book hands over his gun 20 min after fleeing; Macbeth prevaricates to act 2 end; Hamlet "the play's the thing"). The Roadmap explains it: act 2 IS the movement from refusal to acceptance.
- Inciting incidents need not be explosions (Fawlty Towers: a disliked guest). Its limit: the first important choice the protagonist makes.
- Fairy-tale (Jack & Beanstalk): sent to sell the cow → swaps for beans, thrown out → beanstalk grows. Inciting incident = invitation to leave home and climb.
- **Structural identity**: act 1's second turning point (inciting incident) and the penultimate act's (crisis) are the SAME device; only position renames it. First half → deeper into the forest; second half → signposts the return.

## Symmetry and mirroring
Fold any archetypal story at the midpoint and the halves answer each other.
```
   ACT1 <-> ACT5  (often chiastic: opening beat rhymes with closing beat)
   ACT2 <-> ACT4  (moves toward commitment / tests it)
   ACT3a <-> ACT3b  around the midpoint
   No knowledge <-> Total mastery
   act2 crisis (mini inciting) <-> act4 crisis (story crisis)
```
- Each side reflects opposite mental states; each outward point mirrored on the return.
- Participants mirror too: Darryl slighted (act 2 end) helps track them (act 4 end); Rosencrantz & Guildenstern attend Hamlet (act 2), die (act 4).

One example (Godfather): act 1 wedding, honest with Kay, father shot <-> act 5 traitor revealed, death, funeral, lies to Kay. Full first-and-last-act tables for four+ films: structure-worked-examples.md §VI.

Work "feels wrong" when it doesn't fit the structure it aspires to; Shakespeare "feels right" because the tripartite shape occurs five times.

### The last-act template (three-part)
1. **Reawakening**: at the worst point they waver until a new "call to action" appears (Don's dying tip; E.T.'s glowing heart).
2. **Re-acceptance**: accept, commit, pursue to the logical conclusion (Michael's baptism massacre; the final chase).
3. **Total mastery**: one final, most dangerous choice to overcome the flaw (Michael lies to Kay; Elliot's goodbye).

## Multi-protagonist stories
Same rules; the baton passes, each character carrying a component of the single master shape.
- American Graffiti: four teens, each with a call; one character's midpoint embodies all.
- TV gang show (West Wing, ER pilot): all components present, each carried by a different character (inciting → one, midpoint → another, climax → a third).
- Pulp Fiction reorders three scrambled stories into one five-act shape (moving Vincent's death earlier manufactures death-and-rebirth).
- Strands connect by subject, precinct, interaction, theme, or any mix; even Red Plenty assembles the same shape.

## Vital caveats
- Ideal patterns, not laws. Great works subvert deliberately, drawing power from the archetype (No Country; The Wire's Omar works because it's narratively "wrong").
- Many successful works are simply imperfect (Lion King lacks act 3's second half / act 4; Raiders has seven acts). Structure serves the story's needs.
- Don't write from a chart. The archetype arises unconsciously. Write dialectically (McGovern: first with passion, then with your head). Use this file to DIAGNOSE, not paint by numbers.
- Rules must be learned before broken. Sorkin: "The real rules are the rules of drama... Aristotle."

## Authoring checklist
1. Define the flaw (3D) or knowledge deficiency (2D) and the threatened "home"; make the want tangible (object/person) and primal (love, survival, revenge, belonging, self-esteem).
2. Build act 1 as a mini-story; inciting incident = its crisis point, an invitation into an alien world built from everything the protagonist lacks; act climax answers "how will I deal with it" (the what, then the how).
3. Optionally defer full commitment to act 2's end (deferred call), where a second inciting incident forces "things can't stay the same."
4. Place the moment of truth exactly halfway: first embrace of the missing quality (or the adversary revealed); pair gain with escalated jeopardy (risk/reward); make it the point of no return.
5. Make the act 4 crisis the worst possible consequence of the act 1 decision, shadowed by death; pose "revert and die, or change and live?"
6. Make the act 5 sub-goal identical to the original goal; antagonist embodies the flaw (external = internal); run the three-beat last act (new call, committed pursuit, hardest choice); never skip/underplay the obligatory climax.
7. Mirror act 1 in act 5 (ideally chiastically), act 2 in act 4, act 3's halves around the midpoint; mirror participants; pay off every first-half question in the second half.
8. Resolve every setup in the denouement; reward/punish per the moral design (reward change, punish it for tragedy, twist it for irony).
9. Give the antagonist "equal rights" (a story is only as good as its counter-argument; fullest treatment in tv-and-series-structure.md "Theme").
10. For tragedy, run the Roadmap of Change in reverse: corrode a good quality along the same beats until the flaw's opposite is reached.
11. For multi-protagonist work, keep the single master shape and pass the baton across characters (inciting, midpoint, climax).
