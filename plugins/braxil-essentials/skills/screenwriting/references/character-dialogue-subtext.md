# Character, Dialogue, Exposition, Subtext

Source: John Yorke, "Into the Woods" (chapters: Character and Characterization, Character and Structural Design, Character Individuation, Dialogue and Characterization, Exposition, Subtext).

One principle underlies everything in this file: the juxtaposition of a thing with its opposite. Character is facade set against flaw. Structure is want set against need. Dialogue is word set against deed. Exposition is information set against conflict. Subtext is text set against intention. Character and structure are indivisible: one is a manifestation of the other. When two opposites are juxtaposed correctly, an explosion occurs and story comes alive.

## Character vs characterization

### The basic principle: identical yet different

- We are all identical, yet we are all different. Everyone shares the same basic psychological make-up (the ability to love, to be jealous, to procreate, to be defensive, to be open, to be vengeful, to be kind) and displays those characteristics in different proportions depending on who and what we are. Just as all humans look the same yet are totally different, so with psychological make-up.
- Everyone customizes, consciously or not, everything they do: clothes, car, home, phone. We all do similar things, yet everybody does everything in their own unique manner. That is the raw material of characterization.

### Character is revealed through choice under pressure

- In every scene a protagonist meets a mini crisis (a subversion of expectation, a blow to their established plans) and must choose a new course of action. Each choice reveals a little more of who they are. When a character ceases to move, they cease to be revealed; the audience ceases to be active and so ceases to care.
- The standard definition still holds: offer everyone the same 10 million and each will spend it differently. Everyone surmounts the same obstacle uniquely and leaves fingerprints behind. This applies to ANY obstacle: we all make coffee, eat, and drive differently.
- **The Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy opening (Arthur Hopcraft, 1979)**: four men enter a meeting room, and every entrance characterizes without a word. Esterhase, prim and precise, arranges his folder just so and checks his fob watch. Bland backs in unkempt, coughing, cigarette in mouth, ignoring everyone. Alleline enters brusquely and takes the head chair acknowledging no one. Haydon drifts in effete, balancing a teacup, tries to shut the door with a back heel, fails, and sits unconcerned; Esterhase gets up to shut it. Study this scene: it is pure characterization through behaviour under a trivial shared obstacle (entering a room and sitting down).
- **The House of Saddam opening**: Saddam Hussein summons his best friend, embraces him, pulls a gun and shoots him in the head. One piercing direct action tells us, wordlessly, the kind of man he is: he wants to show the Iraqi people he is utterly ruthless, and if he can do THAT, his logic runs, he is capable of anything. Yet he is also capable of great love for his family. In that paradox lies the truth about characterization.

### The root of all character: image at war with feeling

- **The conflict between how we wish to be perceived and what we really feel is at the root of all character.** Nixon, in Oliver Stone's film, staring at Kennedy's portrait: "When they look at you, they see what they want to be. When they look at me, they see what they are."
- Two writer's thought experiments for finding that conflict fast. Your husband commits adultery; he is President of the United States; a voracious press invites you to comment: what do you say? You and your younger brother compete for leadership of the Labour Party; you are far more experienced and everyone expects you to win: how do you feel if you lose, and what do you say to him? The gap between the honest answer and the public answer IS the character.
- The animal beneath the citizen: we are animals capable of rationality, with personal survival to ensure yet a society to live in, so we restrict what we feel and would want to say. Remove identity (an internet message board, an anonymous blog) and the inner animal comes off the leash; strip away the need for a tolerant, understanding appearance and you unleash the impotence, anger and rage that can lurk beneath. No one is immune: publicly we present ourselves as models of civic virtue (in France, no one supported Vichy; in South Africa, no one ever voted Nationalist). We deplete our energy managing perception; it is an anxiety that fuels fashion, music and art, and even its inverse (the "guilty pleasures" phenomenon: the liberation of ceasing to be cool and dancing to Abba).
- Real towering figures show the same dissonance: Stalin professed love for a people he killed by the millions; Blair proclaimed socialism while expanding free-market capitalism; Kennedy's idealism did not extend to women or Vietnam; Steve Jobs was an anti-materialist hippie who capitalized on a friend's giveaway inventions; Reagan, "America's friend," lived an almost solitary private life.
- Montaigne: "We are, I know not how, somewhat double in ourselves, so that what we believe we disbelieve, and cannot rid ourselves of what we condemn." St Paul (Romans 7:19): "For the good that I would, I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do."
- Practical consequence: characters are most interesting, most exciting to write, and most true to life when they lie, self-sabotage, and act contrary to their conscious proclamations and beliefs.
- **The Butch Cassidy cliff scene (Goldman)** as the model: posse behind, torrential river hundreds of feet below. Butch reasons through options; Sundance, the fearless gunfighter, refuses the jump, escalates to "Get away from me! I wanna fight 'em!", and only when cornered ("You wanna die?!") confesses, wildly embarrassed: "I can't swim!" Butch guffaws: "Why, you crazy, the FALL'll probably kill ya!" A whole character (image of invulnerability, hidden shame, and the safety of admitting it only to his partner) delivered inside an action beat. McKee: Butch and Sundance "are two sides of one personality. They are one protagonist."
- Chris Rock on why relationships are hard to maintain: "you can't get nobody being you. You got to lie to get somebody... When you meet somebody for the first time, you're not meeting them. You're meeting their representative!"

### The psychological basis (why the paradox is universal)

- **Maslow** (A Theory of Human Motivation, 1943): the hierarchy of needs. The primal urges (food, water, sex, security, self-esteem, self-actualization) are selfish manifestations of the drive to survive. But seeking safety, ironically, requires REPRESSING those very urges so one can live within a group: unbridled desire or thirst for revenge would jeopardize the security sought. That repression creates the conflict between the way we want to be seen and the deeper feelings we are reluctant to admit.
- **Freud**: the battle between super-ego (the parent: rational, ordered) and id (the child: irrational, animal, libidinous).
- **Jung**: every psychological power has its opposing force; most pertinently, the persona (the facade shown to the world) versus the shadow (the unconscious urges beneath). The persona must encounter and integrate the shadow.
- **Erikson**: normative conflict (source of the identity crisis). **Adler**: individual psychology (source of the inferiority complex). **Rollo May**: existential psychoanalysis. All these theories differ in detail but agree on two things: man lives in a conflicted, neurotic state in which primal desires war with socially acceptable behaviour, and those neuroses must be integrated and overcome for "happiness" to be achieved.
- This is exactly the shape of the archetypal story: a flawed, conflicted hero journeys toward completion, integrating the lessons learned on the way. Happy endings, in fiction and in psychology, mean resolving conflict and balancing opposing forces. Fitzgerald: "The test of a first-rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in mind at the same time and still retain the ability to function." He might as easily have said a first-rate human being.

### Build every major character on a paradox

Julie Goodyear: "It's not a smile, it's a lid on a scream."

- **House**: a misanthrope who will go to any lengths to save a life.
- **David Brent** (The Office): believes everyone loves him, to hide the terrifying loneliness within; his desperate loneliness fuels his desperate presentation. The fake-documentary format was the masterstroke: the camera gave licence to show both the real selves and the facades; the show's very subject is how neurotic, unhappy people WISH to be seen.
- **Basil Fawlty**: a man of substance, versed in the finer things, proud of his service, contemptuous of the working class; also a struggling philistine, a hen-pecked husband running a cheap Torquay hotel.
- **Captain Mainwaring** (Dad's Army): soldier, public figure, model of respectability; also a figure of fun, a provincial bank manager in a hopeless marriage.
- **Don Draper** (Mad Men): rich, sophisticated playboy who has everything; also dirt-poor "white trash," a lost soul who has nothing, and not actually Donald Draper at all.
- **Michael Corleone**: continually at war not just with others but with himself; the midpoint image of a man who believes himself one thing battling a very different inner self.
- **Mark Zuckerberg** (The Social Network): Trent Reznor scored him as a plangent melancholy piano over an insistent throbbing electronic edge: loneliness plus underlying rage.
- **Glee vs Mad Men**: two hit shows from the same society at the same time, mirror images of each other. Glee is about liberating the inner self from the repression of peer pressure; Mad Men represses all human feeling for the holy grail of appearance. Same programme, flipped on its head.
- Internal conflict can be split across two characters: Butch and Sundance, Lethal Weapon, 48 Hrs., every buddy cop pairing. In extreme form, one character splits into two identities: Superman, Spider-Man, Captain America, Batman (The Incredible Hulk as the dark inversion), Harry Potter, and, uncannily like the darkest Batman, Dexter (a serial killer as superhero for our times). The superhero formula is a ready invitation for a child's empathy: someone who looks familiar but secretly harbours an omnipotent god.

## Want, need, facade, flaw (character and structural design)

### The four terms

- **Want**: a superficial conscious desire for the thing the character THINKS they need in order to present themselves to the world; a projection of how they consciously wish to be seen. Kane wants power; Gatsby wealth; Tony Soprano "respect"; Rocky the World Championship; Lightning McQueen the Piston Cup. These bolster the outer shell, the mask, the super-ego: the "character" they want others to see. What are Facebook profiles but adverts for how we would like to be seen?
- **Need**: the unconscious, deeper requirement that would actually make them whole. They fear losing because loss would expose the soft underbelly, the id. What they are scared of, ironically, is the part that would make them whole.
- **Facade**: constructed of traits the character believes beneficial but that will, as we discover, destroy them. **The facade triggers the problem.** Lightning McQueen is happy to be perceived as arrogant and indifferent, and those very traits land him in his predicament. William Thacker (Notting Hill) is the opposite type, but his diffidence, shyness and reserve are exactly what stand between him and Anna Scott. Jack Baker's selfishness loses him Suzy Diamond in The Fabulous Baker Boys. Michael Corleone's compassion leads him to avenge his father.
- **Flaw and hidden strength**: the traits a character believes to be a weakness (if they are conscious of them at all) become the elements that offer redemption. McQueen's selflessness and empathy save his soul; and, because The Godfather is a dark inversion, it is Corleone's ruthlessness that ensures his succession.
- **The antagonist is the embodiment of the very thing lacking in the protagonist.** The "monster" they must overcome externalizes the internal gap.

Worked examples of the full quartet:

| Character | Want (conscious) | Facade (believed beneficial) | Flaw or buried trait | Need (would make whole) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lightning McQueen (Cars) | the Piston Cup | arrogance, indifference | selflessness, empathy | connection, humility |
| William Thacker (Notting Hill) | Anna Scott | diffidence, shyness, reserve | capacity to act and declare | courage to claim love |
| Charles Foster Kane | power | the empire builder | the boy deprived of love | to love and be loved |
| Jay Gatsby (tragedy) | Daisy, via wealth | the invented self | the poor boy underneath | authenticity (never embraced) |
| Michael Corleone (dark inversion) | to stay clean of the family | the war-hero civilian | ruthlessness | in the inversion, the flaw "wins": succession |

Everything above the waterline is presentation; everything below is the true engine of the story. Drama is the process by which pressure forces the submerged part to the surface.

### Egri's seeds: plant the conflict from the first scenes

Lajos Egri: "Every character a dramatist presents must have within it the seeds of its future development." There must be the potential for villainy at the start in the boy who turns criminal at the end.

- Jack Baker is a selfish screw-up unable to cope with intimacy, but from the opening we see he looks after the little girl upstairs, fights passionately for his sick dog, and, as the woman he sleeps with reminds him, has "great hands." The potential to love is on screen before the story needs it.
- Michael Corleone rejects his family's credo of violence, but the STEEL with which he explains the family to Kay reveals the seeds of his future self.
- Internal conflict exists from the beginning of the story, like tinder, ready to be kindled into flame.

### How the arc maps onto the five acts

Timing rules (learn these cold):

1. The **need becomes conscious at the inciting incident**.
2. The **need is embraced at the end of act 2**.
3. At the **midpoint the need triumphs over the want for the first time** (this is a rather good definition of a three-dimensional midpoint's function): Thelma's look of post-coital revelation; the look of both pain and power on Michael Corleone's face. The subconscious has been dredged and brought to the surface.
4. **Post-midpoint the protagonist must integrate** the now dominant new self with the old one. It is not enough to assume an entirely new personality: they must merge the good from the new with the good from the old.
5. As the journey nears its end, **the two sides are brought into balance**: thesis and antithesis seeking synthesis.

### The proportions idea (25/75)

It is absurd to represent characters mathematically, but as a working tool: Thelma begins her journey 25 percent woman and 75 percent little girl, and ends 75 percent woman, 25 percent child within. In a perfectly ordered script the proportions shift incrementally act by act, one side supplanting the other as thesis meets antithesis.

- **Elle Woods** (Legally Blonde): the costume tracks the internal proportions. She starts all pink with a loud hairdo; ninety-six minutes later, a qualified lawyer, her hair is immaculate and there is a small splash of pink on her collar. Watch how each act changes the mix (and the wardrobe).
- **Macbeth**: the poison inside him grows act by act until it overwhelms him.
- The general law: what appear to be random quirks of characterization are intimately linked to a pattern built on the war between opposites, seeking symmetry. The qualities a character displays in pursuit of their goal will be the ones that sabotage their ability to achieve it, and those traits have initially unconscious opposites that, if redemption is the endpoint, come to their salvation.

### Tragedy: the reversal

- In tragedy the pattern inverts: the character cannot abandon the ego-driven goal for the more satisfying need, and is punished.
- **Jay Gatsby**: creates an entirely fictitious self designed to win Daisy Buchanan, but the very impulses that create his fantasy are the ones that push her away.
- **Ziggy Sobotka** (The Wire, season 2): the damaged, inadequate son of a respected union activist, unable to live up to his father's reputation, tries to replenish his ego by convincing himself and others he is a major-league criminal. He is as inept at crime as at life; the more his plans go awry, the more he becomes a joke, and the derision further aggravates his need to prove himself. Unable to reconcile who he wants to be with who he is, his ending can only be tragic.

## Character individuation

### One flaw, infinite characters

A man with a small penis builds a skyscraper; a woman who fears sex becomes a nun; a man riddled with self-loathing becomes a comic. Melodramatic tropes, but each holds a truth: people construct a public face to deal with the conflicts raging inside. Father Greg in Priest is a practising homosexual; Boardwalk Empire's puritanical enforcer of prohibition is a sexual deviant.

The problem: if everyone with hidden vulnerabilities simply grew a hard exterior, there would be very few character types. Yet Witness, The Searchers and Notting Hill all centre on protagonists terrified of intimacy, and John Book, Ethan Edwards and William Thacker are worlds apart. How can characters with the same root flaw, strapped to the same structural grid, be so different?

Two answers:

1. Writers **dress** their characters differently: behavioural quirks, felicity with language, milieu, setting.
2. More fundamentally, **conflict manifests through different ego defence mechanisms**. Wants are desires characters mistakenly believe will complete them and calm the conflict within: Kane seeks to control a world that terrifies him; Gatsby wants wealth to hide his terrible poverty; Tony Soprano exudes violence to mask his vulnerability; Don Draper builds a false persona to bury the different person underneath.

### Ego defence mechanisms: the individuation toolkit

Freud argued that when id impulses (sex, revenge) collide with the super-ego (this is unacceptable), anxiety and neurosis develop; the ego creates defence mechanisms, not permanent paths to happiness but coping systems for managing day to day. Kenneth Tynan: "a neurosis is a secret you don't know you're keeping." The pioneering catalogue is Sigmund and Anna Freud's; mechanisms are commonly grouped in four categories:

| Category | Example |
|---|---|
| Pathological | delusional projection |
| Immature | passive aggression |
| Neurotic | hypochondriasis |
| Mature | humour, altruism |

The ten with instantly recognizable dramatic manifestations:

| Mechanism | Definition | Dramatic example |
|---|---|---|
| Intellectualization | concentrating on the non-emotive aspects | Manhattan |
| Repression | repelling pleasurable instincts | The Remains of the Day |
| Regression | retreat to an earlier stage of development | The Big Chill |
| Sublimation | shift of negative emotions into another object | Chocolat |
| Rationalization | specious reasoning away of trauma | Leaving Las Vegas |
| Isolation | separation of feeling from ideas and events | The Searchers |
| Projection | attacking others for a fault within the self | The Sopranos |
| Denial | refusal to acknowledge emotional trauma | Rosemary's Baby |
| Displacement | shifting internal aggression to a different target | Death Wish |
| Reaction formation | believing the opposite of what one feels | Crash |

### The practical individuation question

A flawed character has an extraordinarily wide range of options out of which to construct a facade. When differentiating characters who share a flaw (say, inability to be intimate), ask: how does THIS character react to it? Do they:

- Intellectualize it?
- Blame their partner?
- Make a joke of it?
- Live in isolation?
- Bury all desire?
- Deny there is any problem?
- Behave childishly?
- Sleep with prostitutes?
- Build a skyscraper?
- Attack others?

Fear of intimacy alone can prompt all those masks: that is why The Searchers, Witness and Notting Hill are so different yet products of the same underlying flaw. Then dress the result: speech, quirks, milieu.

In story terms, the ego defence mechanism is the mask the character wears when we first meet them, the part of the character that will, if the archetype completes, slough away. The archetype acts as a template for resolving neurotic conflict: that is how deep the psychological roots of story shape go.

### The Rubber Ducky moment (origin of the flaw)

All three-dimensional characters are flawed when we first meet them: there is a mismatch between wants and needs; they cope through defence mechanisms that help short term but damage if sustained. Often the trauma originates in an off-screen experience before the story began.

**Origin of the term**: film director Sidney Lumet and writer Paddy Chayefsky christened it the "Rubber Ducky" moment, their slang for an incident in earlier life that supposedly "explains" who the character is now. The coinage was cynical: it was too easy to explain psychopathy by revealing that "someone stole their rubber ducky when they were a baby." The cynicism has not stopped it becoming a common motif with real pedigree.

The moment in one-line formulas:

- Kane was deprived of love, so he builds an empire.
- Louise is hard, aloof and controlling because of what happened to her in Texas.
- Rick Blaine is rude and selfish because Ilsa abandoned him in Paris.

**Canonical examples** (one line each):

- Thelma & Louise: Louise was raped in Texas (hence hard, aloof, controlling).
- Citizen Kane: his separation from "Rosebud" (deprived of love, so he builds an empire).
- Casablanca: Ilsa leaving Rick in Paris (hence rude and selfish).
- Rain Man: the brothers split up as babies.
- The Silence of the Lambs: Clarice witnessing the slaughtering of the lambs.
- EastEnders: Kat's rape by her Uncle Harry.
- Walk the Line and Ray!: the early death of a sibling (one shown in prologue, one in flashback).

**Placement**: most stories withhold the moment until at least the end of the fourth act, partly to create mystery and anticipation, partly because the story IS the character's journey toward that moment: confronting and coming to terms with the cause of the trauma is what lets them move on. The Rubber Ducky moment is when the split in personality originally occurred, the schism between the healthy individual they were born as and the damaged person they became: the event that catalysed the defence mechanisms and therefore the facade.

**Periodic structure**: it is also the foundation stone of films where we know nothing about the protagonist at the beginning, and change is created not within the character but by the slow revelation TO THE AUDIENCE of who they actually are. Change occurs not in the protagonist, but in us. Art-house: I Know Where I'm Going!, Red Road. Mainstream: Rachel Getting Married, The Bourne Supremacy. All are structured around the build to, and revelation of, the moment that tells us who the protagonists ARE.

**Warnings**:

- At worst it produces overblown melodrama, speechifying and cliche. Mamet calls it the "death of my kitten" speech: the point three-quarters through when the writer interrupts the action with a pretty monologue beginning "When I was young I had a kitten..."
- Simon Stephens sees the same apprentice tendency: ancient family secrets revealed four-fifths through, often in a drunken confessional speech; he calls it "theatrically inert."
- Orson Welles himself called "Rosebud" "dime-book Freud." Quint's USS Indianapolis speech in Jaws steers a very fine line between power and indulgence.
- **The working rule**: revelation of off-screen trauma works when it informs the current storyline and creates an active goal (Thelma & Louise); it works far less well as mere back-story. For a compelling modern spin, The Social Network can be read as one long argument between the characters about what the Rubber Ducky moment actually is.

**The case for minimal backstory**:

- Mamet: when the film turns narrative rather than dramatic, the viewer's interest is lost; "the garbage of exposition, backstory, narrative, and characterization" literally stops the show. Our only interest should be in what happens next.
- After twenty-three films we know almost nothing of James Bond's background. We do not need to: he is pure character; we know who he is.
- The less back-story a character has, the more readily the audience identifies with them: the more we can see they are like us and not like someone else. Not knowing keeps us watching. Herge's Tintin has no discernible character amid a rich gallery of archetypes precisely so a child can step into his plus fours.
- David Fincher on Travis Bickle: "I don't know where Travis Bickle is from or what made him do what he did. I don't know and I don't care. That's what makes him compelling." If a film works correctly, the protagonists ARE us; the less we know about them, the more (like the characters in Being John Malkovich) we climb into their heads and make their experiences our own.
- Truly archetypal drama induces peace: the story works as a temporary balm, purging our own inner distress; we heal as we watch because we enact the process ourselves. Badly placed back-stories inhibit that process and disrupt empathy. If character and structure are one, there is no need for explanation.

## Dialogue and characterization

Goldfinger: "Do you expect me to talk?" / "No, Mr Bond. I expect you to die."

### Every utterance is an expression of intent

- Every utterance, however intangible, is at some level an expression of intent. The three most important functions of dialogue (characterization, exposition, subtext) are ALL products of character desire. Dialogue is both born out of, and an essential component of, structure.
- David Hare argues paradoxically that "telling, not showing" is the truer way, that film is as much a verbal as a visual medium. He is right, but not for the reasons he thinks: verbal felicity matters and dialogue can make or break a work, but that does not make film a verbal art. Great dialogue works because telling IS showing: it reveals character.
- The Kuleshov Effect applies to dialogue too (a realization dating from the arrival of sound with The Jazz Singer, 1927): audiences infer a causal relationship between image and dialogue exactly as between image and image. Dialogue builds the facade (unless their guard is down, people speak according to how they would like to be seen), but masks drop, and truer intent is glimpsed when words are juxtaposed with action. When a character says one thing and does another, drama comes alive, because a gap opens in which the audience can be active.
- **Good dialogue is a manifestation of behaviour, not an explanation of it.** Speaking is another form of doing: a tool a character uses to negotiate an obstacle. Like any action, each utterance leaves a fingerprint. Dialogue should be the character IN action.

### Dialogue is not conversation and not narrative

- Transcribe a real TV interview verbatim and it is a mess of shorthand, revisions and interrupted thoughts. Good dialogue does not resemble conversation; it presents the ILLUSION of conversation, subservient to the demands of characterization and structure.
- Dialogue is not narrative either: it does not carry the story. Dialogue is the characters' responses to the narrative, their reactions to the obstacles that litter their path.

### Whedon's law: everyone has a reason

Joss Whedon: "Everybody in your scene, including the thug flanking your bad guy, has a reason. They have their own voice, their own identity, their own history. If anyone speaks in such a way that they're just setting up the next person's lines, then you don't get dialogue: you get soundbites... if you don't know who everybody is and why they're there, why they're feeling what they're feeling and why they're doing what they're doing, then you're in trouble."

### Voice differentiation axes

- The levers: **grammar, vocabulary, syntax, rhythm, sentence length, jargon or slang**. Combined in a particular way they tell us who a person is. Change one and the character changes.
- It is not just what someone says; HOW they choose to say it matters as much. Every utterance reveals something of the melting pot of desire, culture, background, worldview, status, social codes, gender, subconscious fears and upbringing: the crucible from whence they came. These choices may be subconscious, but they are still active choices, some made long before the story began.
- Dialogue must convey how a character WANTS to be seen while betraying the flaws they want to hide. Comedy pushes this to the extreme: David Brent; Ali G (closely modelled on rap DJ Tim Westwood, son of an Anglican bishop).

### Red flags

- Reflective dwelling on back-story; over-explanation.
- The words "do you remember?" or "that reminds me of when..." are the sound of a piece in trouble.
- Dialogue fails whenever it stops being an accurate, characterized reaction to an obstacle in pursuit of a goal.
- Jed Mercurio: "Dialogue is the least important element of my writing. A lot of new writers spend an inordinate amount of time polishing dialogue to try to fix problems, when the problem is much more likely to lie in structure or character." Great dialogue is an art, but structure remains its bedrock.

## Exposition

Exposition is TELLING in a medium built on SHOWING: form and function are fundamentally at odds. It rarely occurs in real life, which is why it is the easiest thing to write badly and the hardest to write well. Bad exposition is the first thing that betrays a writer; mastery of it is one of the great tells of talent.

### A short history

- Fifth century BC: Euripides' Prologue, often a deity in human form, imparted all setting, biography, character and motivation. The Prologue was the narrator.
- As realism grew, a character standing outside the action became anomalous. Shakespeare dabbled with passing gentlemen commenting on the action (Henry VIII's First, Second and Third Gentleman); by Victorian times the job fell to two maids gossiping about their employers, tradecraft fondly known as "table-dusting."
- The device died when craft made it possible to present all required information dramatically, and thus surreptitiously. Terence Rattigan is arguably the first absolute master of modern realist exposition: The Winslow Boy conveys a theft, a trial and a national scandal seamlessly in reported action without ever leaving the drawing room.
- The cautionary tale: early UK continuing drama (the 2005 Holby City scene Yorke quotes) forced writers to explain everything ("It's her faulty valve, isn't it? The baby's putting too much pressure on her heart, like you said it would"), on the belief the audience was not very bright. The results were turgid, and pure Acorn Antiques satire fodder.

### The core dilemma

Most dramatic fiction demands plausibility: characters must not say things they would never say in real life. Yet characters must also impart essential information for the audience's benefit. Just as the writer resolves the conflict between structural demands and "reality," the same circle must be squared with dialogue. The answer, once again, lies in embracing the principles of dramatic structure.

### The two types

1. **Information neither audience nor characters know.** The reason for imparting it can be explicit.
2. **Information the audience needs but ALL the characters already know.** The dangerous one: the reason must be made underlying, which is to say it must be SHOWN.

### The techniques

- **The questioner device** (type 1). Doctors and policemen dominate dramatic terrain partly because they legitimately elicit plot information. EastEnders opened in 1985 with the murder of Reg Cox partly so the police interrogation could give the audience access to the Square. Most new series carry an ingenue: Carter in ER, Rachel in Friends, Sam in Life on Mars, any first-year student in Casualty. Tourists, students, strangers, authority figures: they provide the dramatic imperative for facts to be explained. They are us.
- **Create a reason through desire** (type 2). A wife would never tell her husband "Listen, you know you've got..." about an illness they both know he has. The better writer plumps for "Are you completely moronic? For Christ's sake, see a doctor, it's cancer." Desperation, the need to repeat something because it is so important, supplies a REASON, and where there is a reason there is character desire. All exposition, like all dialogue, is driven by desire; bad exposition is easy to detect because that imperative is absent.
- **Disguise exposition with conflict.** Desire should always be countered by an opposite desire. Yorke's own Westminster Abbey demo. Without conflict: MAN A: "Isn't that the Prince Regent, only son of Queen Victoria, heir to the throne and a man whom I'm told consorts with prostitutes, standing over there?" MAN B: "Yes." (Dead on arrival.) With contradictory goals: MAN A: "A fine man." MAN B: "Hardly. He's a disgrace to his mother." MAN A: "Dare you blacken the Queen's name?" MAN B: "If Victoria knew her Prince Regent was a perpetual client of whores, she'd speak no more highly of him than me." Same facts, now invisible. Rule: **exposition works when it is a tool a character uses to achieve their desire; the greater the conflict, the less visible the exposition.**
- **Give the information impact.** Exposition automatically becomes more interesting if the investigating policeman is talking to the culprit, or the doctor hates telling bad news. The Prince Albert facts would carry even more force if Man A were Albert's best friend or secret lover.
- **Emotional overlay.** Mercurio (Cardiac Arrest, episode one): a terrified junior doctor stammers through breaking the news of a death ("he wasn't a well man... we gave him injections... oxygen... nebulizers and I... we did everything we could") in a scene of agonizing discomfort, so painful the widow still has to ask "how is Albert now?" The emotional impact renders the exposition invisible; disguise exposition with emotional overlay and it becomes undetectable.
- **Move or cut.** Ask where each fact is genuinely NEW to someone on screen and impart it there; deliver consequences later when they land. The modern rewrite of the Holby scene: most of Julia's condition would have been imparted earlier (new to the patient), most consequences after the ultrasound; the only information the scene really needs (Julia must have an emergency ultrasound) can be conveyed in few or no words: the nurse spots an anomaly, the patient looks worried, cut to the ultrasound being performed. Even better if the nurse tells Julia there is no need to be concerned.

### The Apocalypse Now briefing: all techniques married

In one deft first-act scene, Willard is called before his commanding officers, and Milius and Coppola get across almost every fact the film needs: who Kurtz was ("one of the most outstanding officers this country has ever produced"), what he has become (the snail-on-a-straight-razor tapes, the Montagnard army worshipping him like a god), the mission ("terminate the colonel's command... with extreme prejudice"), and the world's moral rot. How:

- Every line carries each character's very clear intention, often masked.
- The interrogation opening ("Did you not assassinate a government tax collector in Quang Tri province?") is a TEST: by seeing Willard's eyes as he delivers the formal denial, we know both that he did it and that he is proving his credentials BY denying it. Word juxtaposed with image gives meaning beyond words: subtext.
- Conflict and unease run under the surface: the commanders are up to the same dirty tricks they are projecting onto Kurtz; Willard's "Yes, sir, very much so, sir. Obviously insane" is clearly unsure; Lucas is uneasy giving the order.
- The deeply personal (the tapes of Kurtz's own voice, Corman's "there's a conflict in every human heart between the rational and the irrational") gives every fact impact and emotional overlay.

Study this scene whenever a briefing, case-opening or mission-assignment scene must carry heavy information.

## Subtext

### Definition and source

- Walter Cronkite announcing Kennedy's death: the phrase ("President Kennedy died at 1 p.m. Central Standard time") juxtaposed with the action (glasses removed, a pause, glasses effortfully replaced, duty resumed) lets the audience infer the truly epic nature of the news. The Kuleshov Effect in action; the power lies in what is concealed.
- Ted Tally (The Silence of the Lambs): "What's important is not the emotion they're playing but the emotion they're trying to conceal."
- Stanislavski asked his students to seek an underlying intention or objective in every line: what is said is rarely what is meant. The real meaning of dialogue is not literal; it may lie in a battle for status, control or power, or in a desire to express love, sadness or loss.
- **Definition: subtext emerges from the interaction between a character's facade and their actual intention or goal.** Under pressure to express true feelings, characters struggle to keep the mask up. As want collides with need, the gap between what characters say and what they do is revealed, and that gap is the stuff of drama.
- Extreme example: Little Mo to Trevor (EastEnders, 2001), immediately after he has raped her on the bathroom floor: "I love you." Not truth: an active desire for her own safety, words used to quell the immediate threat. She hates him but is too scared to say it.

### Safety governs masking

- Every character comes into a scene with a desire. How openly they express it depends on whom they are talking to, their emotional state and where they are. All characters seek safety: is it safe to declare the intention, or is there a safer way to achieve the goal?
- Safety is subjective: for one character it means being the highest status person in the room; for another, the lowest; for another, only being with their spouse. Security allows overt expression. Insecurity (an arch-rival, a violent husband, a new girlfriend) means intention is masked, and subtext occurs.
- Reasons for masking range from terror of exposure to the desire to manipulate others. The screenwriter's job is to convey what is underneath in a way that tells us who the character really is. Sometimes desire is fully overt (Michael Corleone makes no bones about deciding to murder Sollozzo): then there is no subtext, and none needed.

### The masking ladder

Every character attempts, for example, seduction differently. A man walks up to a woman at a bar (the four steps, verbatim):

1. MAN: I'm going to take your clothes off.
2. MAN: I really like you.
3. MAN: Bit chilly for the time of year, isn't it?
4. MAN: Think you're special, don't you?

```
   INTENT (constant): seduction
   ================================
   | 1. "I'm going to take your   |  <- naked intent,
   |     clothes off."            |     zero mask
   |------------------------------|
   | 2. "I really like you."      |  <- softened,
   |                              |     lightly masked
   |------------------------------|
   | 3. "Bit chilly for the time  |  <- fully deflected,
   |     of year, isn't it?"      |     heavily masked
   |------------------------------|
   | 4. "Think you're special,    |  <- inverted into
   |     don't you?"              |     aggression
   ================================
    more mask = more audience
    inference = more character
```

The further down the list, the more the intention is masked, though the underlying one remains the same. Audiences infer not only the true intention but a good deal about the character: Man 4's almost naked aggression suggests a hinterland of troubles and concerns.

### Two kinds of facade

1. **Conscious deception of others**: the gang in The Ladykillers; Ash, the duplicitous android doctor in Alien.
2. **Unconscious deception of the self**: the richer seam, the product of even greater conflict and its ego defence mechanism. The Curse of Steptoe: the fictional Harry H. Corbett, terrified of not being taken seriously, cannot accept in a TV interview that he just makes people laugh, and rambles about politics, sex, general economics and the Church ("it's certainly not about... er... the rag and bone business"). He is not aware of the desire he is masking.

Georg Simmel: all we communicate to another individual is "a selection from that psychological-real whole whose absolutely exact report... would drive everybody into the insane asylum." Without subtext you have a flat, linear world: everything literal, everything spoken meant. With subtext the writer accesses the gap between language and thought, and playing there comes much closer to truth.

### Rules and warnings

- **Subtext is not elitist; it is the hook.** Bad producers fear it because it requires interpretation, but interpretation is precisely how the audience becomes an active participant. Whatever a person's IQ, they can pick up on a nurse telling a patient "everything's fine" when it is not. Yorke watched junior-script-editor meetings replace every line carrying inherent meaning with a literal one: the blood slowly drained out of a living work.
- **Never let characters explain their own motives**: they automatically sound false. Few but the pathological let truth shine straight through their own facade, and, as Montaigne noted, "No description is as difficult as the description of self." True self-knowledge is gifted to the very few; it is the ENDPOINT of a story, not a tool to aid authorial exposition. Explanation kills drama, as does the impulse to make everything everyone says immediately clear.
- **Audiences do not need to understand every word; they need to infer intention.** No one needs to know how to perform surgery to enjoy ER or Holby; they only need the surgeon's goal (save the patient) and may relish the jargon. Viewers work out meaning from context like schoolchildren decoding unfamiliar words. The Sting's con-man patter explains nothing and works perfectly. David Simon: the belief that an audience needs spoon-feeding has one outcome, every character talking the same way (EastEnders once briefly banned first regional and then even cockney accents, forgetting that Auf Wiedersehen, Pet and Minder revelled in dialect alien to most of their audience).
- **Voice-over narration almost uniformly fails** because the narrator tells you everything: no subtext, nothing for the audience to DO. Pushing Daisies, A Very Long Engagement and One From the Heart push you away despite ravishing visual beauty; telling has replaced showing and the audience is made redundant.
- **The joyous exception: unreliable narrators.** Orson Welles in F For Fake is wonderfully devious; the supreme case is Gulliver's Travels, where the growing gap between what Gulliver does (ends up living in a stable, happily communing with a horse) and how he describes it (pure "rationality") dramatizes his madness, made more horrifying by his complete inability to recognize his own condition. The gap IS the drama: the Kuleshov Effect again.

### The thematic endpoint: King Lear

King Lear tells the story of a man who, on condemning Cordelia for speaking as she feels, learns the cost of the gap between speech and thought. As protagonists journey toward completion they learn to heal the duality in their nature: inner and outer worlds, want and need, facade and flaw, and, finally, word and deed. When Lear learns, in Edgar's words, to "Speak what we feel, not what we ought to say," he realizes the horrible truth about facades and the need to reconcile them with truth. Dialogue, with its ability to mask as much as it reveals, is as indispensable a part of structure as character, act division, inciting incident and story.

## Quick checklist

1. Every major character: what facade do they present, what flaw does it hide, and which ego defence mechanism is the mask? Is the paradox between image and behaviour sharp enough to state in one sentence (House: misanthrope who saves lives)?
2. Is character revealed through choice under pressure in every scene, and could a stranger characterize each person from how they merely enter a room (Tinker Tailor test)?
3. Want vs need: want conscious and image-driven; need surfaces at the inciting incident, is embraced at the end of act 2, triumphs over want for the first time at the midpoint, integrates with the old self post-midpoint, balances at the close. In tragedy, the character fails to make the trade and is punished.
4. Antagonist = embodiment of the very thing lacking in the protagonist.
5. Egri's seeds: are the traits that will save (or damn) the character planted, visibly, in the first scenes?
6. Same flaw, different mask: have you chosen HOW each character reacts to their flaw (intellectualize, blame, joke, isolate, deny, regress, sublimate into work, attack), then dressed them in distinct speech, quirks and milieu?
7. Rubber Ducky: if you reveal origin trauma, does it inform the current storyline and create an active goal (not mere back-story)? Withheld until at least the end of act 4? Have you considered revealing nothing at all (Bond, Bickle)?
8. Every speaker, including the henchman, has a reason, a voice, an identity and a history; no one exists to set up the next person's lines.
9. Voices differentiated on grammar, vocabulary, syntax, rhythm, sentence length, jargon; dialogue is reaction to obstacle, never conversation transcript, never story-carrier.
10. Red flags absent: no "do you remember?", no "that reminds me of when...", no polishing dialogue to fix what is really a structure or character problem.
11. Exposition: every fact delivered as a tool of someone's desire, countered by an opposing desire, with impact and emotional overlay; anything that cannot earn that is moved to where it is new to someone on screen, or cut to near-wordlessness.
12. Every scene runs on masked intention scaled by how safe the character feels; the mask slips only under pressure.
13. No character explains their own motives; self-knowledge arrives only at the end. No omniscient voice-over (unreliable narrators excepted).
