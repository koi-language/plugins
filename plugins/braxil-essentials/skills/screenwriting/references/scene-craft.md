# Scene Craft: Beats, Turning Points, Showing not Telling

Source: John Yorke, *Into the Woods*. Writing the scenes five-act structure is built from.

## Drama is life with the dull bits cut out

Hitchcock. The writer distils: select only significant moments, present them in a linked chain of cause and effect. Seek key moments; distil, concentrate, chisel, hone.

Every unit of drama = a **unit of change** at a magnification: **Story** = complete change (full arc); **Act** = major change; **Scene** = minor individual change; **Beat** = one action met by one reaction. Scenes are the single cells; effort concentrates there. **If nothing changes across a scene, it is not a scene. Cut it.**

## Fractal principle: every scene is a story in miniature

The same shape repeats at every scale, story→act→scene→beat (owned by story-structure.md "Fractals"). Scaled to the scene, scenes are built like stories: **set-up → conflict → crisis → climax → resolution**, with story elements scaled down: protagonist → whoever drives the scene; antagonist → whoever blocks; inciting incident → the intrusion starting the confrontation; journey/conflict → the chain of beats; crisis → the unexpected reaction (turning point); climax+resolution → often cut, played out in the following action.

Consequences: all story-structure knowledge applies inside every scene, scaled down; test a scene like a story (who wants what, what blocks, where it turns, what changes); all structure at every scale is **question/answer** (first half asks "what's the worst consequence of this decision?", second half answers "this is").

## Scene protagonist & antagonist: equal and opposite desires

**Every scene has a protagonist and antagonist with equal and directly opposite desires; only one can win.** Rules:
1. Per scene: who wants what, concretely, right now, and who/what blocks it with equal force.
2. Scene protagonist ≠ story protagonist: any character can drive a scene; the film's antagonist can be a scene's protagonist.
3. Can't name both desires → no engine. Rewrite or cut.
4. The winner wins via a **turning point**; executing them well is a tell-tale sign of a writer's ability.

## Beats: action and reaction

A **beat** = one action/reaction pair. Both pursue their equal-and-opposite desires beat after beat until one crucial moment:

`ACTION→REACTION→… → UNEXPECTED REACTION (turning point) → NEW WANT ("what now?") → set-up of next scene`

Write beats as genuine exchanges of pressure (each action pushes on the other's desire, each reaction pushes back). The chain must **escalate**; two beats at the same intensity → cut one.

## The unexpected reaction: the turning point

**Scenes exist BECAUSE of their turning point**: it's why the writer chose that moment; turning points are the units of change. Action/reaction continues until the **unexpected reaction**: one character achieves their goal, the other loses it (EastEnders: Kat's "Yes I am!" mother-reveal turns scene, episode and lives in one line). The turning point:
- Corresponds to the **crisis** in act and story; like any crisis it demands a **choice**.
- The answer to that choice ("what will they do?") = the **set-up of the next scene** (three-act structure at cellular level).
- Forces change by confronting the character with the consequences of **NOT** changing.
- **The right road must be the harder one, risking everything** (Kat's honesty exposes her deepest secret).

## Scene crisis = mini inciting incident

Scenes are **mini inciting incidents linked** into a story; each crisis is a mini explosion upsetting the character's life and creating a new want to solve it.
- Inciting incidents aren't confined to opening acts: fractally they recur at the end of every act and inside every scene.
- Structurally identical: inciting incident = act turning point = midpoint = crisis point = scene turning point = **a choice presented to a character**; only the name changes with position.
- The character goes "into the woods" every scene: solid ground → goal → world thrown into turmoil → "how do I get out of that?"

**Cliffhangers are crisis points** (imagine the drums ending every scene); the drums are structurally identical to the internal unexpected reaction. Discipline: imagine the duf-duf ending each scene, ask what earns it. **Subversion of expectation** is the essential engine of all archetypal drama at every scale: led to expect one thing, turn the corner to its opposite (thesis met by antithesis).

## Come in late, get out early

Goldman: enter at the last possible moment, leave the instant it's done. In a sound scene three parts can be cut: **set-up** (implicit from the previous scene), **climax** and **resolution** (both played out in the following action). A scene can be **confrontation only**. **ENTER** at the last possible moment; **CUT** ideally right on the crisis. Done well it's a never-stopping chain of confrontation/crisis, a French elision dropping unnecessary letters.

**Top-spin**: cutting on the crisis creates powerful narrative drive. Every scene ends on a question: "where did that come from?" and, crucially, "how do they get out of that?" Each ending needs an explanation → curiosity, anticipation, deferred gratification. Forster: story's one merit is making the audience want to know what happens next, its one fault the reverse. Lee Child: ask a question, self-consciously withhold the answer. Not meretricious but narrative: all story is repeated question/answer, and "come in late, get out early" accelerates it by cutting each scene at its worst point.

## Showing, not telling

**Bad writing explains; good writing shows.** Structure = presenting images so the audience must work out the relationship between them (don't say "she's in a mood," show "she's tapping her foot").
- **Kuleshov Effect**: one actor shot intercut with soup/coffin/girl reads as hunger/grief/desire: given disparate images an audience assembles meaning (basis of film grammar).
- **Two plus two** (Stanton): give the pieces, not the answer; audiences work for entertainment and are thrilled to solve it themselves. Works with words too, not just images.
- **Diegetic vs mimetic**: diegetic **tells** ("she was boring"); mimetic **shows** (describe the symptom, let the reader draw it). Film/TV are mimetic (juxtaposed images convey mental states); theatre is diegetic (no close-ups; soliloquy does the work of the cut): stage writing on screen is turgid. Carry story in the **order of images**, not dialogue.

**Dramatizing interior life**: the moving image can't show the unseen (Forster: in drama all happiness/misery must take the form of action or stay unknown). Express interior life as action, inferred from context:
1. **Convey mental states by juxtaposing images**: can't write "David wonders how to vote"; precede "David stares into the fire" with a scene undermining his certainty, and the audience infers it, more powerfully for the work.
2. **Reveal character through behaviour; never explain motivation**: given a clear goal, *how* they act reveals them; stop moving and they stop being revealed, the audience stops caring.
3. **Trust the audience to work**: viewers like being trusted to make connections; condescending drama that explains all, offers no ambiguity, flattens every voice, fails.
4. **Confrontation of opposites is the base unit**: something confronted with its opposite recasts our notion of it; every scene's crisis is a microcosm of an inciting incident; juxtaposing opposites (verbal/visual) is the central plank of showing, of all humour, of all narrative.

**Peripeteia & anagnorisis** (subverting the audience, not just the character): present facts, invite an inferred connection, reveal the opposite (the "victim" lives; the protagonist was dead; the hunter is the hunted; the narrator is the killer). Aristotle's **peripeteia** (reversal: "a change to the opposite by probability or necessity") always paired with **anagnorisis** (discovery: ignorance replaced by knowledge). The soap cliffhanger ("You ain't my mother"/"Yes I am") is its direct descendant.

## Putting it together: chaining scenes into acts

Every scene ends on a mini inciting incident, so scenes chain into acts and acts into the story; the same question/answer motor runs at every scale.
- A crisis embodies the **worst consequence** of the decision taken at the explosion (the master question/answer formula and its Macbeth illustration: story-structure.md "Question and answer").
- Structure conspires to bring the protagonist face to face with their darkest fear and force them to confront it at the crisis.
- The same tripartite cell changes purpose by **position**: story start → inciting incident; middle → midpoint; end → climax.

Sequencing:
1. Track the **want** each turning point creates: it must be visibly picked up as the next scene's set-up, or cause-and-effect snaps.
2. Audit the chain as **questions and delayed answers**; every answer should detonate a fresh question before the audience settles.
3. Payoffs mirror set-ups: whoever is slighted in the first half returns in the second. Plant each element with its mirror scene in mind.

## Common scene failures

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Flat, nothing happens | No opposed desires; only one wants | Give an equal/opposite goal |
| Characters agree, swap info | Exposition wearing a scene's clothes | Make the info a weapon in a conflict |
| Nothing follows | No turning point → no new want | Find the unexpected reaction or cut |
| Drags at the start | Set-up written out, not implied | Enter at the last possible moment |
| Fizzles at the end | Climax/resolution on screen | Cut on the crisis; next scene answers |
| Characters explain feelings | Telling, not showing | Convert to action/juxtaposition |
| Twist lands with a shrug | Four given, not two plus two | Withhold the conclusion; give the pieces |

## Compressing and expanding time (edit-aware)

Spend screen time only on interest/meaning/emotion; trim in the script first (less dialogue plays better); if a beat can be implied by the cut, don't write it. Writing-side, **come in late/get out early** does this structurally by deleting set-ups and resolutions. The shot-side toolset (dissolve, reveal, cutaway, jump cut, exit-frame jump) is cinematography — `cinematic-video-prompt-engineer`'s domain, not the writer's.

## Surprise vs suspense (Hitchcock)

Tension depends on what the audience knows (bomb under the table): ignorant of the bomb → **surprise** on explosion (~15s shock); sees it ticking while characters don't → **suspense** throughout (~15 min). Decide per scene **who knows what**: withholding from the audience buys a twist (peripeteia); giving them more than the characters buys suspense. Choose per scene, not by habit.

## Scene checklist

1. Scene protagonist and concrete want, right now?
2. Opposition with equal and opposite desire? Only one wins.
3. Do beats escalate as true action/reaction pairs, each adding pressure?
4. Where is the unexpected reaction (turning point), and what choice does it force?
5. Is the character's right road the harder one, risking everything?
6. Does the crisis work as a mini inciting incident, creating a new want feeding the next set-up?
7. Can you enter later? Leave earlier (cut on the crisis)?
8. Would the ending earn the drums (duf-duf test)?
9. What changes? If nothing, cut it.
10. Interior life shown as action/juxtaposition (two plus two), never explained?
11. Decided who knows what, audience vs characters (surprise vs suspense)?
12. Does it end posing a question the audience wants answered?
