# Scene Craft: Beats, Turning Points, Showing not Telling

Source: John Yorke, "Into the Woods" (chapters on Fractals, Scenes, Putting It All Together, and Showing and Telling). How to write the individual scenes that the five-act structure is built from.

## Drama is life with the dull bits cut out

"Drama is life with the dull bits cut out" (Hitchcock). A writer's job is to distil: select only the most significant moments of a life and present them in a linked chain of cause and effect.

- Jack Crabb in Little Big Man is 121 years old and the film lasts 139 minutes: the writers have about sixty seconds per year of his life, and of course they do not spend it that way, because that is not the job.
- Seek out the key moments; distil and concentrate; chisel and hone. Out of less, capture a whole.
- Every unit of drama is a unit of change, at a different magnification:

| Unit | Kind of change it shows |
|---|---|
| Story | Complete change (the full dramatic arc) |
| Act | Major change |
| Scene | Minor, individual moments of change |
| Beat | A single action met by a single reaction |

- The writer's effort concentrates on scenes: they are the single cells of the organism. Select and build these units correctly and a tiny fraction of a life captures the essence of its whole.
- **If nothing changes across a scene, it is not a scene. Cut it.**

## The fractal principle: every scene is a story in miniature

Stories are built from acts, acts from scenes, scenes from beats, and every one of these units has the same shape. Yorke's analogy is a photograph of a tree: remove scale and context and you cannot tell twig from branch from trunk. (Pollock's "chaotic" paintings are fractal in exactly this way: tiny sections mimic the structure of the whole.)

Scenes are internally structured exactly like complete stories: they move from set-up to conflict to crisis, then build to climax and resolution. Every scene contains all the essential elements of story.

| Story element | Its scene-level equivalent |
|---|---|
| Protagonist | Scene protagonist (whoever drives this scene) |
| Antagonist | Scene antagonist (whoever blocks them) |
| Inciting incident | The intrusion that starts the confrontation |
| Journey / conflict | The chain of beats (action and reaction) |
| Crisis | The unexpected reaction (the turning point) |
| Climax + resolution | Often cut, played out in the following action |

### The nesting sketch

```
+------------------------------- STORY --------------------------------+
|  set-up . inciting incident . midpoint . crisis . climax . resolution |
|   +----------------------------- ACT ------------------------------+ |
|   |  set-up . inciting incident . crisis . climax . (resolution)   | |
|   |   +--------------------------- SCENE -----------------------+  | |
|   |   |  set-up . confrontation . crisis . climax . (resolution) |  | |
|   |   |   +------------------------ BEAT ---------------------+  |  | |
|   |   |   |               action  ->  reaction                |  |  | |
|   |   |   +----------------------------------------------------+  |  | |
|   |   +----------------------------------------------------------+  | |
|   +------------------------------------------------------------------+ |
+------------------------------------------------------------------------+
   Same shape at every magnification. Remove the labels and you cannot
   tell whether you are looking at a beat, a scene, an act or the story.
```

Consequences of the fractal rule:

- Whatever you know about story structure applies inside every scene, scaled down.
- A structurally sound scene can be tested exactly like a story: who wants what, what blocks them, where does it turn, what changes.
- All structure at every scale is question and answer: the first half of any unit asks "what is the worst consequence of this decision?" and the second half answers "this is."

## Scene protagonist and antagonist: equal and opposite desires

Drama demands conflict on a scene by scene level. **Every scene has a protagonist and an antagonist with equal and directly opposite desires, and only one of them can win.**

- It is not enough for Lauren Branning in EastEnders to want a drink: Kat must refuse to serve her.
- It is not enough for President Bartlet in The West Wing to want to get out of bed: his wife must forbid him.
- The goals of scene protagonist and scene antagonist are in direct conflict. Opposites once again.

Rules:

1. Decide, for every scene: who wants what, concretely, right now, and who or what blocks it with equal force.
2. Scene protagonist does not mean story protagonist. Any character can drive a given scene; the antagonist of the movie can be the protagonist of a scene.
3. If you cannot name both desires, the scene has no engine. Rewrite or cut.
4. For the winner to win, the scene needs a turning point. How well turning points are executed is a tell-tale sign of a writer's ability.

## Beats: action and reaction

Scenes break down into even smaller units called beats. A beat is one pair of action and reaction: one character does (or says) one thing, and the other responds. Both characters keep pursuing their equal and opposite desires, beat after beat, and the scene proceeds action/reaction/action/reaction until one crucial moment.

### The beat chain

```
 ACTION -> REACTION -> ACTION -> REACTION -> ACTION -> ! UNEXPECTED REACTION !
 |______________|      |______________|                          |
     beat 1                beat 2        ...                     v
 (desires locked in direct opposition)                    TURNING POINT
                                                       one character achieves
                                                       their goal, the other
                                                       loses it; a choice is
                                                       forced on the loser
                                                                 |
                                                                 v
                                                             NEW WANT
                                                       ("What are they going
                                                          to do now?")
                                                                 |
                                                                 v
                                                    SET-UP OF THE NEXT SCENE
```

- Write beats as genuine exchanges of pressure: each action must actually push on the other character's desire, each reaction must push back.
- The chain must escalate. If two consecutive beats apply the same pressure at the same intensity, cut one.

## The unexpected reaction: the turning point

**Every scene has a turning point for one simple reason: scenes exist BECAUSE they have a turning point.** It is why the writer selected that moment of the character's life at all. Turning points are the units of change.

All scenes proceed on the basis of action/reaction/action/reaction until they suddenly hit an unexpected reaction: the moment when one character achieves their goal and the other loses it. That is what a turning point is.

### Worked example: EastEnders, Kat and Zoe (Tony Jordan)

Context: two sisters have been arguing. Zoe wants to go to Spain with her Uncle Harry; Kat has forbidden it. Fuelled by drink, the row bursts out of an Indian restaurant into Albert Square. Zoe storms out with Kat in pursuit:

| Line | Beat label |
|---|---|
| KAT: Zoe, come here! | opening pressure |
| ZOE: No! I'm fed up with you picking on me all the time! | counter-pressure |
| KAT: I'm not picking on you. | |
| ZOE: Embarrassing me in front of everyone. | |
| (Kat catches her up in Bridge Street.) | |
| KAT: Just listen to me, will you? | ACTION |
| ZOE: I'm going and there ain't nothing you can do about it. | REACTION |
| KAT: D'you wanna bet? We'll go and ask Dad together, shall we, see who he listens to? | ACTION |
| ZOE: Why don't you just leave me alone? | REACTION |
| KAT: Because you're not going to Spain! | ACTION |
| ZOE: Yes I am. | REACTION |
| (Zoe moves to stride away; Kat grabs her arm; Zoe spins and pushes her off.) | |
| ZOE: Get away from me! | |
| KAT: No! | |
| ZOE: You don't rule my life. | |
| KAT: You're not going to Spain and that's that. | ACTION |
| ZOE: Why not? | REACTION |
| KAT: Because I said so, alright? | ACTION |
| ZOE: And I have to do everything you say, do I? | REACTION |
| KAT: No ... but ... | ACTION |
| ZOE: (cuts in) You can't tell me what to do, you ain't my mother! | REACTION |
| (Zoe turns, this time striding away.) | |
| KAT: (shouts after her) Yes I am! | UNEXPECTED REACTION |
| (Zoe stops dead in her tracks and looks back at Kat. FADE OUT.) | |

Both characters pursue equal and opposite desires (Zoe: leave; Kat: stop her), trading action and reaction, until Kat's "Yes I am!" detonates: the unexpected reaction. One line, and the scene, the episode and the characters' lives turn.

### What the turning point does

- The scene turning point corresponds to the moment of crisis in act and story. Like any crisis point, it demands of the protagonist a choice.
- The answer to the question posed by that choice ("What are they going to do?") forms the set-up for the next scene. Three-act structure reproduced at cellular level.
- Turning points force change because they bring a character face to face with the consequences of NOT changing. Kat is presented with the prospect of losing Zoe unless she tells the truth (macro parallel: Kaffee in A Few Good Men will not bring down Jessup unless he grows up).
- **The road the character should take must be the harder one, the one that risks losing everything.** Kat's harder road is darker still: not only is she Zoe's mother, but (unknown even to the audience) the sainted Uncle Harry raped Kat years before to become Zoe's father. Choosing honesty may force her biggest secret out.

## Scene crisis = mini inciting incident

What all scenes are built from, in essence, are mini inciting incidents linked together to form a story. Every scene's crisis point is a mini explosion that upsets the character's life, creating within them a new plan (a new want) to solve it.

- Inciting incidents are not restricted to opening acts: in fractal form they occur at the end of every act and inside every scene.
- Structurally, an inciting incident, an act turning point, a midpoint, a crisis point and a scene turning point are the same thing: a choice presented to a character. Only the name changes with position.
- Effectively a character goes "into the woods" in every scene: they start on fairly solid ground pursuing a goal they believe will restore order, something happens to throw their world into turmoil, and they must ask again "How the hell do I get out of that?"

### Cliffhangers are crisis points

Ashley Pharoah (co-creator of Life on Mars, trained on EastEnders): "I didn't know how to write EastEnders until I started to imagine the drums at the end of every scene." The iconic drums that mark each episode's cliffhanger are structurally identical to the unexpected reaction inside every scene. Cliffhangers ARE crisis points; crisis points ARE cliffhangers.

- Useful discipline: imagine the drums (the duf-duf) at the end of every scene you write and ask what earns them.
- Subversion of expectation is the essential engine of all archetypal drama at every scale: we are led down a path to expect one thing, only to turn a corner and be confronted with its opposite. Thesis met by antithesis, in action.

## Come in late, get out early

William Goldman: "I never enter scenes until the last possible moment ... and as soon as it's done I get the hell out of there."

If a scene is structurally correct, three elements can be (and often are) eliminated:

1. **The set-up**: implicit from the previous scene.
2. **The climax**: played out in the following action.
3. **The resolution**: also played out in the following action.

A scene can consist solely of the period of confrontation. Done well, the drama becomes a chain of confrontation/crisis that never seems to stop moving; like a French elision, the unnecessary letters are removed. (Jimmy McGovern uses it endlessly; Sorkin's West Wing "In the Shadow of Two Gunmen" Part Two opens on the revelation that the President has been shot and just keeps escalating.)

### Scene anatomy with trim marks

```
      (implicit in the                                             (played out in the
       previous scene)                                              following action)
  ..................  +---------------------------------------+  .....................
  :                :  |  CONFRONTATION                        |  :                   :
  :    SET-UP      :  |  action/reaction -> action/reaction   |  :  CLIMAX           :
  :   (cuttable)   :  |  -> ... -> UNEXPECTED REACTION        |  :  + RESOLUTION     :
  :                :  |            (turning point / crisis)   |  :   (cuttable)      :
  :................:  +------------------------------|--------+  :...................:
                      ^                              ^
                 >>---+                              +---<<
                 ENTER HERE                          CUT HERE
                 (come in late: last                 (get out early: ideally
                  possible moment)                    right on the crisis)
```

### Top-spin

Cutting away at the crisis point creates what some call top-spin: an extremely powerful narrative drive.

- Every scene ends on a question: partly "Where did that come from?" but more importantly "How are they going to get out of that?"
- Each scene ending requires an explanation, which creates curiosity and anticipation, defers gratification and keeps people watching.
- E. M. Forster: story "can only have one merit: that of making the audience want to know what happens next," and only one fault, that of making them not want to.
- Lee Child: "You ask or imply a question at the beginning ... and you absolutely self-consciously withhold the answer. It does feel cheap and meretricious but it absolutely works." It is not meretricious; it is narrative. All story relies on the repetition of question and answer, and "come in late, get out early" simply accelerates it, forcing every scene to cut off at its worst point.

## Showing, not telling

Screenwriting is showing, not telling. **Bad writing explains; good writing shows.** Structure is the presentation of images in such a way that the audience is forced to work out the relationship between them.

Mike Skinner (The Streets): "How do I express in a picture that she's in a mood with me? I'm not going to say 'She's in a mood with me', I'm going to say 'She's tapping her foot'." We see her face, we see her foot, and we know.

### The Kuleshov Effect

Lev Kuleshov intercut one identical shot of a matinee idol with a bowl of soup, a coffin, and a girl; audiences raved about the actor's effortless hunger, grief and desire. Presented with disparate images, an audience will assemble them into meaningful order. It is a truth on which all film grammar is based. (The VW "Lemon" ad works the same way: the strange juxtaposition of image and strapline sucks you in and impels you to do the work.)

### Two plus two (Andrew Stanton, Pixar)

"Good storytelling never gives you four, it gives you two plus two ... Don't give the audience the answer; give the audience the pieces and compel them to conclude the answer. Audiences have an unconscious desire to work for their entertainment. They are rewarded with a sense of thrill and delight when they find the answers themselves."

- The example that unlocked it for Stanton (Ryan's Daughter): the priest warns Rosy "don't nurse your wishes ... or sure to God you'll get what you're wishing for"; hard cut to a tall handsome stranger in silhouette against the sea. Nothing is said; every viewer concludes "he is exactly what she wishes for, and he will bring trouble."
- The principle applies to words as well as images: Sarah Silverman's "I want to get an abortion, but my boyfriend and I are having trouble conceiving" is pure two plus two.

### Diegetic vs mimetic

- Diegetic writing tells: "Kim Wilde was boring." Mimetic writing shows: John Peel reviewed her Wembley set by describing his row's discussion about the catering staff's Motherwell-coloured uniforms, forcing the reader to bridge the gap and draw the picture themselves.
- Film and television are mimetic media: they rely on the juxtaposition of images to convey mental states. Theatre is far more diegetic (no close-ups, little fragmentation of time or space, the soliloquy doing the work of the cut). Stage writing transplanted to screen is boring and turgid because it is not written for the medium.
- Do not carry story in dialogue by default; carry it in the order in which images appear.

### Dramatizing interior life

The moving image cannot deal with what we cannot see. E. M. Forster: "In the drama all human happiness and misery does and must take the form of action. Otherwise its existence remains unknown." All interior life (feelings, reflections, motivation) must be expressed in action and inferred by the viewer from context.

Techniques:

1. **Convey mental states by juxtaposing images.** You cannot write "David stares into the fire wondering whether to vote Labour or Conservative"; the audience has no way of inferring it. Precede "David stares into the fire" with a scene in which something he thought certain about one of those parties is found wanting, and the audience understands mimetically, and more powerfully, because they worked it out themselves.
2. **Reveal character through behaviour; never explain motivation.** Characters do things, and through doing them we understand them. As long as a character has a clear goal, the way they choose to act in achieving it reveals them. When they cease to move, they cease to be revealed; the audience ceases to be active and so ceases to care.
3. **Trust the audience to work.** David Simon (The Wire): the viewer "likes being trusted to acquire information on his terms, to make connections, to take the journey with only his intelligence to guide him." Condescending drama explains everything immediately, offers no ambiguities, and flattens every voice into the viewer's own. "This, of course, sucks."
4. **Use the confrontation of opposites as the base unit.** Something confronted with its opposite makes us recast our notion of that something. The crisis point in every scene is a microcosm of an inciting incident; the juxtaposition of opposites, verbal or visual or both, is the central plank not just of showing over telling but of all humour, all narrative.

### Peripeteia and anagnorisis: subverting the audience

At its most heightened, subversion of expectation happens not just to the character but to the audience itself: present key facts, invite an inferred connection, then reveal the opposite.

- Les Diaboliques: the murdered headmaster is not dead at all; the plot was aimed at the wife all along.
- The Sixth Sense and The Hours: the audience is forced to believe the version presented, then learns the protagonists are dead.
- Oedipus Rex: Oedipus hunts the cause of the plague and finds it is him (same DNA in The Bourne Ultimatum and Planet of the Apes).
- The Murder of Roger Ackroyd: the killer is the narrator; the novel, in hindsight, a suicide note.

This is Aristotle's **peripeteia** (reversal: "a change to the opposite in the actions being performed ... in accordance with probability or necessity"), always partnered with **anagnorisis** (discovery: ignorance replaced by knowledge). It is a fundamental unit of dramatic construction, and the soap cliffhanger ("You ain't my mother" / "Yes I am") is its direct descendant: peripeteia in action.

## Putting it all together: chaining scenes into acts

Because every scene ends on a mini inciting incident, scenes chain naturally into acts, and acts into the story. The same question-and-answer motor runs at every scale.

- A crisis point always embodies the worst possible consequence of the decision taken when the initial explosion occurred. Q: what is the worst consequence of Macbeth killing the King? A: his former allies march on him for revenge.
- The question/answer pair binds acts as well as stories: in Thelma and Louise, the worst consequence of two women stopping at a roadhouse without male company (mini inciting incident) is an attempted rape and a shooting (mini crisis).
- Structure at the level of scene, act and story conspires to bring the protagonist face to face with their darkest fear, and at the crisis point forces them to confront it (commitment-shy Rick faces losing Ilsa in Casablanca; selfish Woody faces losing everyone in Toy Story; timid Will faces what timidity costs in Notting Hill).
- The same tripartite cell takes on a different purpose depending on where it sits: at the beginning of a story it resolves into an inciting incident; in the middle, a midpoint; at the end, a climax. Write the cell well and position gives it its function.

Practical consequences for scene sequencing:

1. Track the want created by each scene's turning point: it must be visibly picked up as the next scene's set-up, or the chain of cause and effect snaps.
2. Audit your scene chain as questions and delayed answers. Every answer should detonate a fresh question before the audience can settle.
3. Expect payoffs to mirror set-ups: whoever is slighted in the first half tends to return in the second (Darryl slighted at the end of act two of Thelma and Louise is hunting them by act four). Plant scene elements with their mirror scene in mind.

## Common scene failures

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Scene feels flat, nothing happens | No opposed desires; only one character wants something | Give someone an equal and opposite goal |
| Characters agree, exchange information | Exposition wearing a scene's clothes | Make the information the weapon in a conflict |
| Scene ends but nothing follows from it | No turning point, so no new want | Find the unexpected reaction or cut the scene |
| Scene drags at the start | Set-up written out instead of implied | Enter at the last possible moment |
| Scene fizzles at the end | Climax and resolution played on screen | Cut on the crisis; let the next scene answer it |
| Characters explain how they feel | Telling, not showing | Convert interior life into action or juxtaposition |
| Twist lands with a shrug | Four given instead of two plus two | Withhold the conclusion; present only the pieces |

## Compressing and expanding time (edit-aware writing)

Spend screen time only on what carries interest, meaning, emotion. Trim in the script first: scenes almost always play better with less dialogue than written.

| Tool | What it does |
|---|---|
| Dissolve | Bridges two states; the audience accepts elapsed time between them |
| The reveal | Start the next shot close, pull back to disclose the change |
| Cutaway | A bridging image that licenses a jump forward |
| Jump cut | Once transgressive, now accepted simply as a scene shortener |
| Exit-frame jump | A character walks out of frame; the audience accepts finding them elsewhere, later |
| Come in late / get out early | The structural version: delete set-ups and resolutions entirely |

Write with the edit in mind: if a beat can be implied by the cut, do not write it.

## Surprise vs suspense (Hitchcock)

Whether a beat is suspenseful or dull depends entirely on what the audience already knows. Hitchcock's bomb under the table:

| Audience knowledge | Effect | Yield |
|---|---|---|
| Audience does not know about the bomb | Surprise when it explodes | About fifteen seconds of shock |
| Audience sees the bomb ticking, characters do not | Suspense through the whole conversation | About fifteen minutes of tension |

Rule: decide deliberately, for every scene, who knows what (characters vs audience). Withholding from the audience buys a twist (peripeteia); giving the audience more than the characters buys sustained suspense. Both are tools; choose per scene, not by habit.

## Scene checklist

Run every scene through this before it survives a draft:

1. Who is the scene's protagonist and what do they want, concretely, right now?
2. Who or what opposes them with an equal and opposite desire? Only one can win.
3. Do the beats escalate as true action/reaction pairs, each one applying new pressure?
4. Where is the unexpected reaction (the turning point), and what choice does it force?
5. Is the road the character should take the harder one, the one that risks everything?
6. Does the scene's crisis work as a mini inciting incident: an explosion that creates a new want feeding the next scene's set-up?
7. Can you enter later? Can you leave earlier (ideally cutting right on the crisis)?
8. Would this scene ending earn the drums (the duf-duf test)?
9. What changes across the scene? If nothing changes, cut the scene.
10. Is every piece of interior life expressed as visible action or juxtaposition (two plus two), never as explanation?
11. Have you decided who knows what, audience vs characters (surprise vs suspense)?
12. Does the scene end posing a question the audience wants answered?
