# Visual Grammar: the Language of Cinema

Source: The Filmmaker's Handbook (Ascher and Pincus, 4th ed.), chapter 9, "Shooting the Movie". This is the grammar of how shots are framed, how the camera moves, and how shots flow from one to the next. Use it whenever you choose a shot type, an angle, a movement, or a coverage plan for a script or a storyboard. Style and content are not separate things: each reflects on the other, and there is meaning in every shot.

## Core units

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Scene | An event that takes place in one setting in a continuous time period. If an actor walks from the kitchen into the dining room and the camera follows, the script often treats that as a separate scene. |
| Sequence | A scene or a series of scenes that make up a unit. Sequences can span different locales that together form a conceptual whole (the baptism sequence in The Godfather: a church intercut with murders across the city). |
| Shot / camera angle | One of the pieces a scene is divided into for editing (a scene may also be a single shot). "Shot" sometimes means the camera take, sometimes the edited portion of a take used in the final movie; context distinguishes. |
| Setup | Each new camera position for a different angle. Changing setups usually means changing lighting too. Simply changing the focal length of the lens from the same position is NOT a new setup. |
| Take | Each run of the camera from the moment it starts until it is turned off. "Scene 8, Take 14" is the fourteenth attempt at scene 8; "Scene 8A, Take 4" is the fourth attempt at the second camera angle (A) of scene 8. |
| Master shot | A relatively wide-angle, continuous take of the entire scene action, filmed all the way through. |
| Coverage | The options: the different camera angles filmed in addition to the master. |
| MOS | A shot taken without sound. Legend: German directors in early-1930s Hollywood called silent footage "mit-out-sprache" (fractured German for "without speech"). |

## Shot vocabulary (the book's precise definitions)

| Shot | Definition |
|---|---|
| Extreme close-up (ECU) | Part of a face or a small object fills the screen: a watch, an eye, a fly. |
| Big close-up | Just a face fills the screen. |
| Close-up (CU) | Shows a detail of the scene; for a person, a head-and-shoulders framing. In a CU, focus on the eyes. |
| Two-button close-up | Everything from the face down to the second button on the person's shirt. |
| Dirty close-up | A close-up that has the back of another person's head or another element in the foreground. |
| Medium close-up (MCU) | Between CU and MS, roughly chest up. |
| Medium shot (MS) | Not too detailed; includes part of the subject, usually head to knee or waist up. |
| Two-shot | A single shot of two actors from the front, showing them from the knees up or waist up. |
| Knee shot | The knees-up variant of the two-shot framing. |
| Long shot (LS) | Includes the whole body of the person in relation to the environment, usually taken from fairly far away. |
| Wide shot (WS) | A wide view of a landscape or space; environment dominant. |
| Establishing shot | A long shot that defines the basic space or locale where events will take place. |
| Reverse-angle shots | Two shots taken from opposite angles. |
| Over-the-shoulder (OTS) | Taken from behind one actor over her shoulder; the standard, relatively objective coverage of dialogue. |
| POV shot | Taken from someone's or something's vantage point: from behind an actor over the shoulder, with the camera at the position of her eyes, or from an extreme vantage such as directly overhead (bird's-eye view). |
| Insert | A close detail of the action within the scene (hands, an object, a note). |
| Cutaway | A shot away from the main action, used to cover discontinuities or to condense the action. |
| Reaction shot | A shot of someone reacting to the main action (the woman in the crowd at the politician's speech). Cheap editing insurance: always get some. |
| Shot/reverse shot (angle/reverse angle) | Conversation coverage with each person alone in the frame in three-quarter profile; edited, one looks screen right, then the other looks screen left. Typically close-ups, often dirty. |
| Deep-focus shot | The whole frame in focus; the meaning of the scene develops in the deep space of the frame (Citizen Kane). |
| Long take | A shot of long duration; the action unfolds in real space, and the shot's meaning comes from filming, not from editing. |
| B-roll | Noninterview material in journalism-style work; often allowed to play a few seconds in sync sound, then the location audio dips down and the picture becomes a bed for narration. |

### How the sizes flow

- The traditional way to break down a scene: long shot to medium shot to close-up. This orients the audience to the physical space, and the progression of increasingly tight shots suggests forward movement, as though the camera is delving deeper into the action.
- When a scene goes wider (MS to LS), the audience expects action on a larger scale: a new arrival in the scene, or a leave-taking from the action (as might happen at the end of a movie).
- Contemporary audiences are comfortable with a wide variety of cutting styles; the traditional relationships are defaults, not laws. A scene can be played entirely in extreme close-up if the story earns it.
- A model of the classic pattern in miniature (Born Yesterday): the scene begins with a two-shot, cuts to a medium shot of one character, then a reverse angle of the other. Orientation first, then alternation.

## Composition

Framing is a way to control viewers' attention: directing them to certain elements, excluding others, and creating an image that is visually satisfying. Composition in motion pictures differs from painting or stills because objects move within the frame (subject movement), the frame itself can move (camera movement), and one shot is edited next to another, creating a new set of tensions and balances through time. A shot often reveals its meaning through motion, so strong film composition can exist without well-composed still frames: a frame that seems off balance at first may fluidly resolve as it develops, through subject movement, camera movement, or editing.

Two working modes: when filming from a script, the action and framing of each shot can be blocked out (planned) before the take; in unscripted work, framing and movement are improvised from what is seen through the viewfinder AND what is seen and heard outside the frame.

### Rules of the static frame

- **Rule of thirds**: avoid placing important areas of interest dead center (it tends to be dull). Position them one third of the screen width from one side or the other. In a close-up or medium close-up, place the subject's eyes about one third of the screen height from the top; the nose then sits roughly centered. Note: a great many well-balanced compositions do not conform to this "rule".
- **Headroom**: the space between the top of the head and the top of frame. Individual shots vary tremendously in how much feels comfortable: heads can nearly touch the top of frame and work, very tight frames may have no headroom at all, and the same headroom that feels natural in a wide shot can feel excessive in the close-up. Headroom, and composition in general, is subjective: go by instinct in each setup.
- **Edges**: place objects and people naturally, either comfortably within the frame or using the edge to cut them off decisively. Never place them so close to the edge that they seem to fight with it. Avoid large dead spaces or losing the subject in a mass of irrelevant details.
- **Backgrounds**: be particularly attentive to what is directly behind the subject: plants that seem to grow out of a person's head, or activity that distracts from the intended focus.
- **Expectations**: compositions create expectations, which can be used to surprise the audience or to confirm or deny what they anticipate. In horror films, compositional imbalance often suggests something scary lurking outside the frame. Gross imbalances that violate conventional composition are now often used deliberately to add flavor.

### Lead room / looking room

When a subject has a definite movement toward one side of the frame, place the subject closer to the edge he is moving FROM, leaving room ahead. Track someone walking right to left: frame him nearer the right edge, with walking room on the left. Someone in profile looking off screen right should be framed nearer the left edge, leaving space on the right. If a long shot continues, the subject can advance in the frame to suggest forward movement, and even exit frame.

### Depth, scale, and space

- Shoot at an angle that reveals as many sides of an object as possible to enhance the feeling of depth: a building filmed head-on reveals one side; shot from an angle, two sides; shot down and at an angle, three sides.
- Use familiar references to establish scale: an enormous boulder seems larger with a person in the frame.
- Hollywood directors frequently use camera angle, movement, and lighting to create deep space in the image, clearly distinguishing foreground from background and excluding large areas of unmodulated black or white. European directors of the 1960s and 1970s often emphasized the flatness of the screen instead: shooting perpendicular to a wall, letting large areas of the frame overexpose or underexpose. Both are legitimate stylistic positions; choose one on purpose.

### TV cutoff, safe areas, aspect ratio

- What you see when framing is often not what the audience gets. Traditional CRT televisions enlarge the picture slightly and crop the edges (TV cutoff or overscan); web videos usually show the entire frame edge to edge; flat panels are in between and inconsistent.
- Avoid positioning anything crucial too close to the edges of the frame (top, bottom, or sides). The **safe action** area marks what may be cropped; the **safe title** area is even closer to center, protecting text that must be readable. Something undesired (a microphone in the corner) may or may not show up depending on the display.
- If you shoot widescreen, the movie may still be shown nonwidescreen (especially broadcast), and vice versa. A widescreen look is often achieved by shooting a less wide format and cropping in post (shoot 16:9, crop to 1.85 or 2.39). If so, shoot a framing chart at the start of production so the intended framing is clearly indicated, and tape the monitor to show the eventual frame.
- Leave room at the bottom of frame if lower-thirds will identify people.

## Camera angles, height, and eye lines

- Camera angles from below suggest the importance, stature, and height of the subject (or may just be unflattering). High angles diminish. Bird's-eye view reads as god-like or extreme detachment.
- Dutch angle (canted, tilted horizon) adds tension to a static frame, often used in medium close-up; it reads as unease or instability. A Dutch head rig (one tripod head mounted perpendicular on another) can even tilt the camera smoothly side to side DURING the take.
- An unleveled tripod produces accidental canted shots and tilted pans; if a horizon is tilted, it should be tilted on purpose.
- Camera height matters: matching the camera to a character's physical position (low at the person on the couch, high from the person standing) quietly assigns the shot to that character's experience.

### The subjectivity ladder (eye line relative to the lens)

The eye line (sight line) is the direction a person is looking relative to the scene and relative to the lens. The angle between the eye line and the lens axis controls how subjective the shot feels. From most objective to most direct:

| Step | Eye line | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Far from lens; profile two-shots and OTS coverage | Objective, observational | Born Yesterday: profile two-shot establishes, OTS medium shots cover the dialogue |
| 2 | Camera physically close to the actors' eye lines; clean singles (no shoulder); camera heights matched to the characters' positions | Heightened sense of sharing the characters' point of view | The Last Picture Show: she is filmed from above (his POV), he from below at the height of her position on the couch |
| 3 | Eye line very near the lens, but not directly into it | Subjective POV | Rashomon: in the duel, each man's shot represents the other's point of view |
| 4 | Directly into the lens | Breaking the fourth wall, or "camera inside the other character's head"; can easily seem awkward in drama | Annie Hall: the master ends with Allen addressing the camera; Lady in the Lake: an entire film shot through the protagonist's eyes |

- Interview craft rides the same ladder: the interviewer sits close to the camera so the subject's eye line is toward the lens but not into it. Errol Morris's "interrotron" is a teleprompter that projects his face onto a screen in front of the lens so the interviewee looks directly into the lens while talking to him. On-camera hosts and correspondents look directly into the lens when addressing the audience.
- **The eyeline match**: a character looks off screen; cut to what they see; the audience instantly reads it as their POV. Rear Window: Kelly and Stewart look out the window (1), cut to Raymond Burr across the courtyard (2), cut back to their reaction (3). One of the strongest tools in the grammar.

## Lens and focus as storytelling

- **Deep focus** (wide lens, everything sharp): the action develops within the frame; meaning comes from staging, not cutting (Citizen Kane).
- **Selective focus** accentuates a portion of the subject. Composition, lighting, and selective focus work together to pull the viewer in (the peephole shot in Psycho makes us want to see what Norman sees).
- **Rack focus / pulling focus** from background to foreground shifts audience attention. Some filmmakers consider the technique mannered unless it follows a movement.
- A tilt-focus lens tilts the plane of focus to isolate a narrow area.
- Lighting can change within a shot to redirect attention (car headlights suddenly illuminating a face).
- **Focal length feel**: wide lenses exaggerate depth and the sense of moving through space, and make handheld jiggle less noticeable; long lenses flatten and compress space and decrease the apparent speed of motion toward or away from camera. Panning with a long lens can even simulate a traveling camera.
- In a close-up, it is usually advisable to focus on the eyes.

## The moving camera

Static (locked-off) shots contrast with moving camera shots. When the camera itself travels through space, the viewer experiences the most distinctly cinematic of shots; it is also often the most difficult and expensive one.

| Move | Definition | When and why |
|---|---|---|
| Pan | Pivot on a horizontal axis, left or right | Best when motivated by a subject moving through space, which makes the rate natural. Pans often cross still landscapes left to right, as though the world unfolds that way. Landscape pans are the hardest: any unevenness shows, and they must be fairly slow to avoid judder. |
| Tilt | Pivot on a vertical axis, up or down | Revealing height, scale, power; following vertical action. |
| Dolly / track (in, out) | The camera physically travels toward or away on a wheeled support | Perspective changes, objects pass by the frame edges, and the viewer feels physically moved into the space. Creates a feeling of depth. |
| Crab / truck (left, right) | Lateral travel, following alongside | Traveling with walking characters; sliding past environments. |
| Boom / pedestal / crane up-down | Vertical travel of the whole camera | A dolly with an integral boom adds enormously to the lexicon of possible shots; cranes and jib arms extend the reach for grand reveals and high angles. |
| Slider | Short rail glides, two to six feet | Even a two-foot slider with a wide-angle lens and a slow, deliberate glide of six seconds or more adds welcome production value to documentary and drama alike. |
| Handheld | Camera carried on the operator's body | Universally read as interiority, subjectivity, urgency, or documentary realism (see doctrine below). |
| Steadicam / stabilizer | Gimbaled arm on a harness isolating the camera from body shake | Dolly-smooth movement with handheld freedom (see below). |
| Zoom | Focal length change during the shot; NOT camera movement | Magnifies the whole image equally, like approaching a still photograph; flattens space; can call attention to the act of filming. Useful to pick out a significant detail. |
| Swish pan | A very fast pan that blurs everything between start and end | Energy, transitions; also conveniently avoids judder because the blur hides everything. |
| Pop zoom | A sudden jump from one focal length to another | Punctuation; deliberate roughness. |
| Dolly-zoom | Zoom one way while moving the camera the other way | Field size stays constant while perspective warps: vertigo, dread, disorientation (Vertigo's staircase; also used in Jaws and GoodFellas). |

### Zoom versus dolly

- Zooming brings the viewer closer to (or farther from) the subject without changing perspective; the entire image is magnified equally. A dolly move changes perspective and passes objects by the frame edges: the viewer physically enters the space. The moving camera creates depth; the zoom tends to flatten it.
- Zooming against the direction of subject or camera movement creates the **treadmill effect**: an actor runs toward the camera while the lens zooms back and seems to make no progress; zoom wider out of a car's front window and the forward motion feels disrupted.
- The classic, graceful zoom starts up slowly, reaches the desired speed, and gradually slows to a stop. If you dislike the zoom feel but need the focal length change, "bury" the zoom inside a pan and it becomes almost invisible.
- Novices are "zoom happy". Zooms are most effective when motivated and deliberate, not random.
- Cutting on zooms: some feel every zoom should come to a stop before a cut, but cuts during a slow zoom often work fine.
- The mechanics shape the feel: a motorized zoom gives the slow, smooth move and must accelerate from a stop and feather back to a stop; a manual zoom puts the operator's hand in direct contact with the "feel", responds faster to changing action, and suits a deliberately rougher style.
- On the treadmill effect and the dolly-zoom, the perspective is what carries the emotion: in Vertigo the camera appears to move down the staircase while the lens zooms back to keep the field size constant, so the viewer sees the same subject with exaggerated perspective and feels the dizziness of height.

### Handheld doctrine

- Handheld arrived with lightweight sync-sound cameras in the early 1960s (after silent-era experiments by Dreyer, Clair, Vigo, and Vertov) and launched both New Wave fiction and cinema verite documentary. At its best it imparts a new electricity to the image.
- The convention that wobbly handheld camerawork equals interiority or a subjective viewpoint is universally accepted, even though in real life our gait and balance keep our view steady. Panic or frenzy can be reinforced by a shaky, nervous handheld style. Horror films notoriously exploit tremulous handheld shots to telegraph the presence of an unseen onlooker.
- Handheld's extreme mobility gives a feeling of intimacy and spontaneity impossible from a tripod or dolly, which is why it is often best for unscripted situations or improvised acting. A skilled operator can also handhold with real steadiness. Audiences are used to an occasional handheld shot mixed in with mostly mounted shots.
- Craft notes that shape what is plausible on screen: wider lenses hide jiggle; walking shots use bent knees and a shuffling gait so the camera does not bob; static handheld frames on landscapes or strong architecture betray every jiggle (put the camera on something instead).

### Steadicam and stabilizers

- A Steadicam blends the freedom of handholding with the controllability of a dolly or crane, and any vehicle (car, boat, helicopter) can serve as a platform for dolly-smooth movement. Pans, tilts, running shots, and stair climbs move with the subtlety of the human body, without jiggle.
- The first Steadicam shot ever seen in a movie (Bound for Glory, 1976) rode a tall crane all the way to the ground, then hopped off and floated through the set: an impossible shot before the device.
- Russian Ark (2002) is a single ninety-six-minute Steadicam take, a virtual encyclopedia of Steadicam timing and moves.
- Character of the movement: Steadicam shots have a floating quality that some find less exciting than well-done handheld; response to unplanned subject movement is slower than shoulder-mounted; holding a stable horizon at the end of a move is a challenge. Wide-angle lenses usually work best when tracking action.
- In many films, fluid movement by Steadicam, slider, dolly, crane, and boom (sometimes in combination) adds flow and lyricism to a scene without representing any particular character's POV.

### The support palette (what each rig can and cannot do)

Storyboard moves that a real support could execute; the constraint is part of the look.

| Support | Gives you | Constraints |
|---|---|---|
| Tripod, fluid head | Smooth pans and tilts; drag makes panning easier | Static position; level it or you get canted shots and tilted pans |
| Geared head | Smooth, repeatable movements for heavy cameras | Heavy; takes experience |
| Hi hat / table stand | Very low angles | Static |
| Doorway / western dolly | Cheap, portable traveling shots | Steerable but cannot move laterally |
| Crab dolly with boom | Travel in any direction plus vertical moves; adds enormously to the lexicon of possible shots | Needs smooth floor, plywood, or track |
| Dolly on track | The smoothest, most repeatable moves; curved track holds a constant distance while circling a subject | Track must be laid, leveled with wedges, and hidden; timing is everything, the track always ends |
| Jib arm / crane | High angles, vertical reach, grand reveals | Jibs are harder to control than built-in booms; industrial cherry pickers can raise a static camera but lack the dampening to end a moving shot cleanly |
| Slider | Short precise glides (two to six feet) | Length-limited by design |
| Handheld | Mobility, intimacy, spontaneity; absorbs vehicle vibration | Shake reads as subjectivity whether you want it or not; heavy over long days |
| Steadicam / stabilizer | Dolly-smooth movement anywhere a person can walk, on any vehicle | Floating quality; slower reaction than shoulder-mounted; horizon control at the end of moves |
| Camera vehicle / tow rig | Tracking shots faster than any dolly; the picture car is towed so actors act instead of driving | Larger car = smoother; constant speed easiest; suction mounts need safety lines |
| Gyro / helicopter mounts | Stabilized shots from boats and aircraft | Specialized rigs (Tyler mount, Wescam) |
| Software stabilization | Post rescue; can make a bouncy handheld shot look dolly smooth | Crops or warps; not a plan, a net |

Even without moving shots, having the camera on a dolly with a boom saves time on set: the camera goes quickly to positions a tripod reaches slowly or not at all. The dolly grip is an extension of the camera operator and needs the same finesse.

### Rules of movement

- **The classic shape of a move**: start from a static position, gradually gain and maintain speed, then ease down to a full stop. Begin and end on a held frame for a few seconds; the editor may cut the statics off, but at least they exist. (The rule is often honored in the breach, and constant-speed moves do appear in films.)
- **Every shot and movement should have a shape**: a beginning and an end. Novices tend to move the camera constantly, which makes footage very hard to cut.
- **The foreground rule**: a camera move reads most strongly when the camera moves past objects in the near foreground. Place foreground elements to accentuate movement; wide lenses emphasize it further.
- **The push-in**: dollying in on a character underscores dawning realization or the seriousness of a moment (the dolly in on Grace Kelly in Rear Window as she realizes Burr may be a murderer).
- **Camera as character**: in Psycho, Hitchcock at times uses moves in which the camera itself almost becomes a character, prowling around a room, manipulating the audience in a carefully calibrated way. The continuous tracking shot that reveals the stolen money and then the packed suitcase creates a tension very different from showing the same objects in separate cut shots.
- **Screen size**: the larger the movie is projected, the more exaggerated every camera movement reads. A quick pan or shaky camera that is fine on a phone can be disorienting in a theater.
- **Directorial questions when planning moves**: How should the audience experience the scene? Should any character be favored in point of view? When should the camera observe from a distance, and when should it be in close? Should it be a voyeur or a participant? Should it lurch impulsively, creep stealthily, or weave with uncertainty? Use blocking to reveal things rather than showing everything up front; use mystery. Some shots are most interesting for what they do NOT show.

## Style: mise-en-scene versus montage

- **Mise-en-scene** (the staging of the shot): in a deep-focus shot or a long take, the whole scene develops within the frame; camera movement, subject movement, dialogue, lighting, and costume all carry the forward movement. Bazin considered concern for the integrity of the photographed space the mark of advanced directors. The visceral proof: Buster Keaton's stunts, performed in long shot, were clearly incredible feats precisely because the space was unmanipulated. The same conviction powers documentary.
- **Montage**: when the action is captured in many shorter shots, the filmmaker controls pacing, directs the audience's attention moment by moment, and can construct entirely new meanings by juxtaposing shots that otherwise might seem unrelated.
- These are two stylistic tools at the filmmaker's disposal, not opposites. A carefully constructed edited sequence can deliver enormous emotional impact; a long master can give the audience a satisfying sense of observing and discovering things on their own.
- Extremes of the master approach: Woody Allen often films dialogue scenes as a single uninterrupted three-minute master with little or no camera movement (Annie Hall, Manhattan), emphasizing performance and writing. The opening of Touch of Evil is an intricately choreographed continuous crane take covering three and a half minutes of action in close-ups and wide shots from high and low angles.

### Classic Hollywood grammar

- Griffith is credited with first exploiting the power of the close-up, creating a new relationship between audience and actor and demanding subtler acting. The silent cinema defined the basic vocabulary of the film image.
- Hollywood sound films until the 1950s: shoot the scene all the way through in a master, then film close-ups as needed. The edited scene opens on the wide establishing shot so the audience is oriented and comfortable before cutting closer.
- From this evolved the traditional four-setup coverage of a two-person scene: **a master shot, a two-shot, a close-up of one character, and a reverse close-up of the other**.
- A radical exception: Lady in the Lake, filmed entirely with a subjective POV camera inside the protagonist's head.
- Since the 1960s the culture and the grammar loosened: free-form shooting that trusts audiences to follow a scene played entirely in ECU; Cassavetes merging documentary and narrative feel; music video's lexicon of quick cutting and wild juxtaposition; deliberately shaky camera, distorted images, intentional jump cuts; handheld "documentary style" drama and mockumentary. Mainstream drama nevertheless keeps its style "invisible", not intruding on the storytelling.
- Best training: watch films and note which scenes work; to isolate the camera-editing relationship, watch with the sound off.

## Point of view in scene construction

Whose experience a sequence follows is a SCRIPT decision before it is a camera decision. Cinema's unique power is letting the audience see the world through a character's experience; how point of view is expressed results from how the script is written, how the director films it, and how it is edited, and must be considered before production.

The book's example: a man goes to the doctor, gets bad test results, goes home. Three coverages of the same events:

| Version | Coverage | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The camera witnesses the whole day with the man: goodbye to his wife, into the building, the doctor delivers the news, questions, telling his wife at home | Close identification with the man and his experience |
| 2 | The camera stays with the wife: she drops him off, drives away, thinks about the possibilities, serves dinner and asks how the checkup went | Her experience of the same events; the office visit is skipped entirely |
| 3 | Open on the doctor alone, reading and reacting to the results; the man enters; possibly no dialogue at all; cut to the man silent at the dinner table, not ready to tell his wife | The audience knows before the character. Every subsequent scene gains irony, tension, or foreboding; emotion arrives through expression and gesture, in relatively little screen time |

Consequences worth internalizing:

- **Restricted POV**: in Chinatown, like many noir and mystery stories, the camera stays with the detective and the audience gathers clues along with him. We have no access to events Gittes does not witness.
- **Audience knowledge changes the value of every shot**: in version 3, a shot of the man in the waiting room is suspenseful because we know the news is bad; in version 1 the identical shot may just be dull. Plan coverage knowing which version of knowledge the audience holds.
- **Hitchcock's law of surprise versus suspense**: a mundane conversation with a bomb under the table that suddenly explodes gives the audience fifteen seconds of surprise. Show the anarchist planting the bomb and setting the timer first, and the same innocuous conversation becomes fascinating: fifteen minutes of suspense, because the public participates in the scene. Welles uses exactly this in the opening of Touch of Evil.

## Continuity style (the illusion of continuous time)

Live TV shoots with several cameras at once, all in real continuous time. Single-camera drama does not: the action is filmed from one angle, then the camera moves, and the action is repeated or restaged, possibly across different days and locations. **Continuity style** is the technique of shooting and editing shots filmed at different times so the action seems to flow continuously: a grammar so familiar it is called "invisible cutting". Some scenes and films demand it; others do not.

### The 180-degree rule

Screen direction is the right or left direction on screen as seen by the audience (a subject facing camera who moves to HIS left moves screen right). The 180-degree rule (the director's line, or simply the line) preserves screen direction across cuts.

- Imagine a line drawn through the main axis of action: a moving car, a football field, the eye line of a conversation. Keep all camera setups on ONE side of that line and screen direction is preserved from shot to shot.
- The canonical illustration: televised football. The blue team moves screen left to right; if a camera on the opposite side of the field is used, the blue team suddenly moves right to left and you are confused. TV crews keep their main cameras on one side and flash "reverse angle" on screen when they break the rule.
- **Legal crossings**: (1) shots ON the line are neutral and cut with either side: someone looking directly into the camera, or the shot from the end zone straight down the axis; (2) the camera may cross the line visibly DURING a take with minimal disorientation, because the audience travels with it. Beware: if that crossing take is dropped in editing, you may be stuck violating the rule.
- Violations may disorient the audience but are rarely disastrous; inserting a neutral shot minimizes disorientation. The rule is critical where screen direction itself constructs the space of the scene: in shot/reverse-shot dialogue, the two people must look in OPPOSITE screen directions at the cut or they stop appearing to talk to each other; in chases, screen direction is what distinguishes pursuit from head-on collision.
- **Conflicting lines**: sometimes two lines of action coexist and one must lose. A couple talks in the front seat of a moving car, shot angle/reverse angle: behind the driver the background moves left, behind the passenger it moves right. The relationship is the key element, so draw the line along their eye line and preserve THEIR screen direction, letting the background contradiction stand.
- **Across scenes**: screen direction applies even to shots that never share a scene. Documentary interviews are classically framed so one subject faces camera left and a subject with a contrary opinion (filmed elsewhere) faces camera right; intercut, the framing itself reads as opposition.

### The 30-degree rule and jump cuts

- Cut from a close-up of character A to another take of the SAME framing of the same person and the discontinuity jumps out: a **jump cut**.
- To avoid it, make a significant change in BOTH the size of the shot and the angle between successive shots of the same subject: a wide shot cuts easily with a medium shot or close-up, but two medium shots of the same person will feel unsmooth. Change size by focal length or camera distance, AND move the camera laterally; the common formulation is that the angle should change by at least 30 degrees.
- Whether a cut feels smooth is also affected by the action, the background, and especially continuity of sound.
- Jump cuts were once a radical grammar that called attention to the medium; today audiences accept them as routine, and filmmakers use them not only to highlight a jump in time but simply because a scene must be shorter and jump cuts do the job.

### Match on action and overlapping action

- When restaging for different angles, the action must be consistent take to take: if the actor picks up the coffee cup on a certain line in one shot, he does it at the same point in every other angle.
- **Overlap the action across setups** even when each part of the action will be filmed from only one angle. Script says: wide shot of a man getting in a car and slamming the door, then a close-up as he starts the engine. Shoot the wide all the way through the slam; then start the close-up from BEFORE the slam, repeating it. The editor can now cut on the action at several different points without discontinuity. Move the camera laterally between the two setups as well; it smooths the cut and hides small mismatches.
- When there is an unintentional mismatch, you can often shoot or cut around it because the audience's attention is focused elsewhere (though there will always be people online trolling for continuity errors). Shooting digital, a continuity question can be settled by checking the previous take.

### Eyeline match

A character looks off screen; the next shot shows what they see, from approximately their vantage; the audience instantly binds the two (Rear Window). The Last Picture Show opens a scene with a POV through a windshield; the cut to the two boys makes shot 1 retroactively theirs, then covers the conversation shot/reverse shot, moving tighter as the scene progresses, the boys looking screen right and the older man screen left.

### Exit frame

- When a character walks off camera, the viewer accepts a time jump: he walks out of frame toward the door, and a cut to him walking down the street or sitting in a restaurant does not feel discontinuous.
- When panning or tracking with a moving subject, let him walk out of frame at the end of the shot: it hands the editor a clean exit and more options.

### Matching state

The same gesture, prop state, wardrobe, weather, hairdo, and light must carry across every angle of a scene: this is the script supervisor's entire job (see the production-craft reference). When boarding, treat prop and costume state as continuity data attached to the scene, not to the shot.

### Continuity without control (documentary logic that generalizes)

- In uncontrolled scenes you cannot restage for new angles, but continuity still rules: always have in mind what shot the present one can cut with; when a cut will be needed, change camera angle AND focal length so continuity editing is easier.
- With repeated action (cooking, chopping wood), cover it from different angles and include overlaps.
- Concealment tricks that buy editing freedom: an OTS from behind the person talking shows the relationship without showing moving lips; shoot phone conversations from angles where the phone blocks the lips; get neutral shots of a musician where no finger positions are visible. Any shot that hides sync detail can be laid over any stretch of the audio.

## Compressing and expanding time

The flow of time is decided partly in the script structure, partly in coverage, partly in the edit. Storytelling balances moments described in detail against action that is highly compressed or omitted; give screen time to what carries interest, meaning, and emotion, and move as fast as possible through necessary but unremarkable story points. Write and rewrite with a close eye to which scenes, dialogue, and details could be condensed or cut.

### Shortening action within continuity style

| Technique | How it works |
|---|---|
| Dissolve | The routine bridge between two distant moments (blank canvas, dissolve, finished painting). |
| Reveal | End shot 1 wide; begin shot 2 on a close-up (her face) and pull back, by zoom or move, to reveal the finished painting. |
| Cutaway bridge | Cut to the painter's cat watching, cut back to the canvas nearly complete. Same mechanism condenses a politician's speech: politician, woman in the audience, politician at a later point of the speech. |
| Jump cut | Now accepted simply as a way to shorten a scene. |
| Exit frame | Subject leaves frame; next shot finds them later and elsewhere. |

### Extending time

- Block the action and cover it so it earns screen time.
- **Slow motion (overcranking)**: the camera runs at a higher frame rate, so slowed action stays smooth and continuous. Slow motion extends real time and gives an event psychological weight. A character's death may occur in an instant yet be the film's most important moment: since Bonnie and Clyde, countless films extend the protagonist's death in slow motion. Riefenstahl's Olympia broke athletes' movements down in slow motion to show what real time hides. Today slow motion is also sprayed on mundane shots just to add feeling; use it with intent.
- Slowing normal-speed footage in post instead repeats frames: motion goes jerky and motion blur becomes visible. Interpolation software helps; if you plan for post slow motion, shoot with a very fast shutter to reduce blur. True overcranking is smoother.
- Scorsese's Raging Bull mixes slow motion, sped-up shots, lingering shots, and very short shots in fast-cut montage; together they extend AND compress time to capture the sensation of being assaulted in the ring.

### High-speed extremes

- Very high frame rates capture or analyze fleeting events: individual water droplets slowly crashing, a bullet shattering glass. High-speed digital cameras shoot thousands of frames per second; strobe lighting acts like an ultrashort shutter.
- Sense of scale: a camera speed of 250 fps stretches ONE second of real time into more than TEN seconds of 24p screen time. Board accordingly.
- High-speed recording means very short exposures, which demand a lot of light. Rapid objects that appear to move slowly but smoothly, clear and distinct with no motion blur, only come from a true high frame rate, never from slowing normal footage in post.
- A side use: when the camera is handheld or on a moving vehicle, faster camera speeds lengthen the distance between jerky movements and make the image seem steadier (subject motion slows too).

### Fast motion and time-lapse

- **Undercranking** (below normal frame rate) speeds motion up. Chase sequences are undercranked (about 16 to 20 fps played at 24) to look faster and more dangerous; the sped-up energy of silent comedy supposedly began as an accidentally undercranked Sennett camera.
- Undercranking also buys exposure in very low light if nothing moves (12 fps gives one stop more than 24); in a dark interior, have actors walk at half speed, or move the camera half as fast, and playback looks normal. Undercranked footage slowed back down in post produces an interesting ghostlike effect.
- **Time-lapse** compresses hours into seconds: the sun sets, a flower blossoms, a building is demolished and another constructed. Do the arithmetic from the desired screen time: a two-hour sunset that should run ten seconds at 24p needs 240 frames, so about 2 frames per minute (single-frame or interval recording). Start before and end after the main action, and record more frames than you think you need. The fewer and farther apart the exposures, the more jumpy or staccato the motion.
- A wide lens usually reads best: wide, traffic becomes a fast-moving river of cars; through a long lens the same scene shows individual cars popping into one frame and vanishing in the next. Walking or dollying the camera a step per frame gives a striking moving-camera time-lapse.
- Very slow per-frame shutter speeds increase motion blur; use it deliberately to turn car lights at night into colored streaks. Decide whether exposure stays fixed (base it on the most important part of the sequence; a sunset on auto-iris can silhouette wrong) or "rides" the changing light.
- **Pixilation and animation** are time-lapse variants: expose a few frames, change the drawing or nudge the object, expose again; on playback, still things move by themselves.
- **Speed ramp**: run normal, then ramp to high speed as the key action begins (cameras can auto-adjust exposure), for fleeting events inside an otherwise normal shot.

## Motion smoothness rules (respect these when boarding fast action)

Motion pictures are an illusion built on still frames; if the change between frames is too great, motion breaks into **judder** (strobing, skipping): the object seems to jump from position to position, and it can give viewers eyestrain.

- At 24 fps there are few images per second, so fast pans and fast subject motion judder. Rule of thumb: allow at least **five to seven seconds** for an object to cross from one side of the screen to the other. This applies both when the camera pans and when the subject moves through a stationary frame.
- Judder is most visible in pans across strong vertical lines; the higher the contrast or sharpness, the worse it gets. Avoid panning across high-contrast scenes with strong verticals.
- If the camera pans WITH a moving subject, the viewer concentrates on the person and is far less likely to notice background judder. Help them: use shallow depth of field, focus on the subject, let the background go soft.
- Fast swish pans are usually not a problem: the blur hides everything.
- Higher frame rates (60 fps) make motion look fairly smooth; judder is a 24 fps problem, and it can also appear at higher rates when an adjustable shutter is set very fast.
- Footage from small-sensor cameras can appear to judder more than large-sensor footage, partly because large sensors make it easy to throw the background out of focus. A slower shutter reduces judder.
- The bigger the screen, the more severe judder appears (the jumps cross a greater physical distance); it also often looks worse in a viewfinder than on a proper monitor.
- Related artifact: wagon wheels on screen that seem stopped or spinning backward happen when exposures catch the spokes at the same or slightly earlier positions in consecutive frames.

### Vehicle speed on screen

| Condition | Apparent speed |
|---|---|
| Shooting along the direction of travel | Motion appears normal |
| Shooting at right angles to the direction of travel | The vehicle appears roughly TWICE as fast as it is |
| Intermediate angles | Between the two |
| Wide-angle lens | Increases apparent speed |
| Long focal length lens | Decreases apparent speed |

Practical vehicle notes that affect what is plausible in a boarded shot: the larger the car, the smoother the ride; constant speed is easiest to shoot, a standing start hardest; shooting at a higher frame rate smooths out unevenness in the ride; real productions tow the picture car on a trailer so actors act instead of driving, and side-window "dolly" moves are shot from the tow vehicle.
