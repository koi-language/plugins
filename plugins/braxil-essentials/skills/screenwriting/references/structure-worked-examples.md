# Worked Examples: Act Structures of Real Stories

Source: John Yorke, "Into the Woods", Appendices I-VII (condensed and restructured for reference use). These are the book's own worked demonstrations of the five-act shape, the roadmap of change, act mirroring, and the equivalence of the rival structure systems.

## How to use these examples

Use these breakdowns as calibration when mapping a new story onto the five acts (see story-structure.md):

1. **Act count is elastic in the middle, fixed at the edges.** Raiders proves the middle section can hold as many acts as the story needs (it has seven plus a coda). What never moves: a first act that is a complete mini-story, a midpoint truth at the exact center, a worst point near the end of the penultimate act, and a final act that mirrors the first.
2. **Locate the midpoint first.** In every example below the midpoint is the moment the protagonist touches the "truth" of the story (proof of guilt, the first kill, the first drink, finding the Ark, the sex inside Malkovich). Everything before is journey toward it; everything after is paying for it.
3. **Check the worst point.** It belongs at the end of act four (or its proportional equivalent). In tragedy it is often inverted into the highpoint of the protagonist's ambition (see Being John Malkovich).
4. **Run the roadmap of change** (the 15-beat progression: no knowledge, growing knowledge, awakening / doubt, overcoming reluctance, acceptance / experimenting, BIG CHANGE, experimenting post-knowledge / doubt, growing reluctance, regression / reawakening, re-acceptance, total mastery). Expect misfits; Yorke flags them himself in Hamlet. The overarching shape matters, not beat-by-beat compliance.
5. **Mirror the first and last acts.** The beats of act one should be echoed, inverted, in act five (Appendix VI below). If your ending does not answer your opening, the opening is probably wrong.
6. **Translate gurus, don't fight them.** If a collaborator speaks Save the Cat or hero's journey, use the equivalence table in Appendix VII; every system is the same engine in different clothes.

None of these works was written to a five-act template. Yorke's point is the opposite: the template is what you find when you X-ray finished work, because the shape is a natural one. "Very few works fit with total accuracy," he notes of Hamlet, yet works written centuries apart in different mediums share the same skeleton, the way all bodies differ but share a frame.

### The generic five-act shape (calibration ruler)

Hold every example below (and every new story) against this ruler. Proportions matter more than page numbers.

### The roadmap of change (stage glossary)

The 15-beat progression used in every roadmap table below. Three beats per act; the shape is symmetrical around the midpoint.

| Act | Stages | What each stage does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No knowledge, Growing knowledge, Awakening | Protagonist ignorant of the story's truth; the truth intrudes; they see it for the first time |
| 2 | Doubt, Overcoming reluctance, Acceptance | Resistance to the call; the push that dissolves resistance; commitment to the journey |
| 3 | Experimenting, BIG CHANGE / KNOWLEDGE, Experimenting post-knowledge | Testing the new world; the midpoint truth acquired; acting on it, often recklessly |
| 4 | Doubt, Growing reluctance, Regression | The cost surfaces; forces mass against the protagonist; retreat toward the old self, ending at the worst point |
| 5 | Reawakening, Re-acceptance, Total mastery | The truth re-embraced; the final commitment; flaw cured (or, in tragedy, flaw triumphant) |

In a dark inversion (The Godfather, Macbeth) the identical beats run with reversed polarity: "mastery" means the flaw has fully consumed the character.

## Appendix I: Raiders of the Lost Ark (seven acts + coda)

Proof that the "middle" of a story can expand into as many acts as the material demands. Each act is a contained unit of gaining, losing, and regaining the treasure, and each ends with a decisive turn that launches the next desire.

| Act | Location / movement | Beats |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Peru, 1936 | Indy seeks a golden idol inside a booby-trapped jungle temple. He gets it, exits, and is confronted by his arch rival, the archaeologist Rene Belloq, who takes the idol from him and leaves him for dead. Indy escapes in a waiting seaplane. |
| 2 | USA | Indy is back teaching archaeology when army intelligence agents arrive: the Nazis are hunting his mentor Abner Ravenwood, believed to hold the headpiece of the Staff of Ra, an Egyptian artefact that can reveal the resting place of the lost Ark of the Covenant. Since the Ark would give the Nazis near-limitless power, Indy is tasked with finding it first. |
| 3 | Nepal | Indy finds Ravenwood dead and the headpiece in the hands of his combative daughter Marion, Indy's former girlfriend. She refuses to sell. Nazi agents invade and burn her tavern to the ground; in the struggle Major Toht burns his hand on the red-hot headpiece, leaving an imprint of it. Indy and Marion escape with the headpiece, and Marion declares that from now on they hunt the Ark together. |
| 4 | Cairo | Belloq is aiding a Nazi dig for the Well of Souls, the Ark's resting place. Using the burn imprint on Toht's hand, the Nazis have built a replica headpiece, but they have miscalculated. Indy, using the real headpiece, finds the TRUE location of the Ark (**MIDPOINT**). The Nazis, having followed him, take the Ark and seal Indy and Marion inside the Well of Souls. They escape. |
| 5 | Airstrip and desert convoy | Indy and Marion follow the Ark to an airstrip where the Nazis plan to fly it out. After killing a giant mechanic, they chase the truck convoy; Indy is shot in the arm but rescues the Ark before it can be shipped to Germany. |
| 6 | Tramp steamer | En route to England, Belloq intercepts them again, this time by submarine. The Ark is stolen once more and Marion kidnapped. Indy dives into the sea and stows away on the U-boat. |
| 7 | Island | Indy tracks them to an isolated island and threatens to blow up the Ark; Belloq calls his bluff and he is captured. Tied to a post with Marion (**WORST POINT**), he is forced to watch the Nazis open the Ark. Knowing the danger of looking at what is inside, he tells Marion to shut her eyes. Demonic forces emerge and destroy the Nazis; the Ark is restored to Indy. |
| Coda | Government warehouse | The Ark is sealed in a wooden crate marked "top secret" and wheeled into a vast warehouse, surrounded by countless identical crates. |

```
RAIDERS OF THE LOST ARK: seven acts + coda

  ACT 1     ACT 2     ACT 3     ACT 4     ACT 5     ACT 6     ACT 7    CODA
[=========|=========|=========|===[MP]==|=========|=========|=[WP]==*|====]
  idol      mission   Marion    Ark       convoy    U-boat    island   warehouse
  stolen    given     joins     found     chase     theft     opening

  [MP] MIDPOINT   : Indy finds the true location of the Ark (act 4)
  [WP] WORST POINT: tied to the post, forced to watch the Ark opened (act 7)
   *   CLIMAX     : the Ark destroys the Nazis; treasure restored
```

### Act one as a complete mini-story

Act one of Raiders is a whole story in miniature, with every structural element present. This is the fractal principle: every act (and every scene) replicates the shape of the whole.

| Story element | Act one of Raiders |
|---|---|
| Protagonist | Indiana Jones |
| Antagonist | Rene Belloq |
| Inciting incident | Discovering the temple that holds the idol |
| Desire / journey | Retrieve the golden idol, surviving every trap |
| Crisis | Belloq surprises him at the exit, armed and backed by warriors |
| Climax | Belloq steals the idol and leaves Indy for dead |
| Resolution | Indy escapes with his life in the seaplane |

## Appendix II: Hamlet, the structural form

Yorke's framing: very few works fit the five-act template Horace prescribed with total accuracy, largely because very few writers work from a precise template at all. What is uncanny is how works written in different centuries and mediums share the same skeleton, the way all human bodies differ but reveal the same frame under X-ray. Hamlet, arguably the greatest dramatic work in English, serves as a metaphor for all of them: each of its five acts contains a major turning point launching a new desire.

**Acknowledged misfits:** Yorke flags moments that do not quite fit. Ophelia's death comes, as we would now expect, at the end of Act 4, but Hamlet has already recommitted to revenge after seeing Fortinbras's army; the beats overlap rather than queue neatly. It seems absurd to draw structural parallels between Hamlet and Thelma and Louise, he adds, but they are there, illustrating the uniformity and universality of the shape.

### Act-by-act synopsis

| Act | Events |
|---|---|
| 1 | Hamlet, Prince of Denmark, sees the ghost of his father the king, who reveals that his brother Claudius murdered him and demands that Hamlet avenge his death. |
| 2 | Unsure whether the ghost is real or a product of his fevered imagination, Hamlet is paralysed into inaction. Spurred by the discovery that Claudius has sent his old friends Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to spy on him, he resolves to prove Claudius's guilt using a troupe of travelling players: "The play's the thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the king." |
| 3 | Coached by Hamlet, the players stage their enactment, and in Act 3 scene 2 (of four scenes: an almost perfectly symmetrical act) Claudius's reaction confirms his guilt (**MIDPOINT**). Giddy with success, Hamlet confronts Gertrude in her chamber and kills Polonius, the king's adviser, hiding the body. |
| 4 | Claudius resolves to ship Hamlet to England and have him murdered mid-passage. On his way into exile Hamlet sees Fortinbras's army marching to die for "a little patch of ground / That hath in it no profit but the name"; fascinated that men will die for something so small, he recommits to vengeance. Ophelia goes mad at her father's death; her brother Laertes returns enraged and is recruited by Claudius into a plot that keeps the king blameless. News arrives of Ophelia's suicide. |
| 5 | Escaped from exile, Hamlet finds himself at Ophelia's graveside ("Alas, poor Yorick"). Laertes confronts him; the fencing match is fixed. Laertes poisons his blade, Claudius laces the wine. Hamlet mortally wounds Laertes but takes a fatal scratch; Gertrude drinks the poisoned wine; Laertes exposes the plot; Hamlet finally kills Claudius before dying himself. Fortinbras arrives, is anointed successor, and declares Hamlet a hero. Order is restored. |

### Roadmap of change applied to Hamlet

| Act | Stage | Hamlet |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No knowledge | Hamlet knows nothing of the ghost |
| 1 | Growing knowledge | Hamlet learns of the ghost |
| 1 | Awakening | Hamlet sees the ghost, who demands revenge |
| 2 | Doubt | Ponders suicide: "I have of late... lost all my mirth" |
| 2 | Overcoming reluctance | Learns Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are spies |
| 2 | Acceptance | Resolves to entrap Claudius using the players ("the play's the thing...") |
| 3 | Experimenting with knowledge | Trains the players with new enthusiasm |
| 3 | **BIG CHANGE (midpoint): KNOWLEDGE** | Entraps Claudius and Gertrude; proves their guilt |
| 3 | Experimenting post-knowledge | Damns Gertrude, kills Polonius, is banished to England |
| 4 | Doubt | Departs for England |
| 4 | Growing reluctance | We learn of Claudius's plan to murder him |
| 4 | Regression | Sees Fortinbras's army; reaffirms his commitment to revenge. Death of Ophelia |
| 5 | Reawakening | "Alas, poor Yorick"; Laertes confronts Hamlet |
| 5 | Re-acceptance | The sword fight. Hamlet kills Claudius. All are slain |
| 5 | Total mastery | Dies knowing his story will be passed on; his body borne off in honour |

Structurally the play is a detective story: the first half is built around the question "Did Claudius murder the king?" and the second around the reaction to the truth discovered exactly halfway through: "He did."

## Appendix III: Being John Malkovich, the structural form

Yorke's framing: it is fascinating that a work so iconoclastic (and brilliant) also clearly conforms to classic structural shape. Charlie Kaufman's riff on the process of empathy is almost totally archetypal: 108 minutes long, act durations almost entirely symmetrical, and the "into the woods" pattern explicit; a journey into another place to find the missing part of oneself is the subtext, if not the surtext, of the film. It does elide beats and steal them across act borders (the portal's discovery arrives in what would classically be act two), yet fits the archetypal mould effortlessly.

**Theme announcement:** the theme is stated by Maxine 51 minutes in, at dinner with the fixated Lotte and Craig: the world divides into those who go after what they want and those who don't; the passionate ones may not get what they want, but they stay vital and die with fewer regrets. When we meet Craig he has nothing; the discovery of Maxine and the portal dangles a new world in front of him where, if he grabs what he wants, everything promises to be his. He journeys to escape his old self, with terrible consequences.

### Act-by-act synopsis

| Act | Events |
|---|---|
| 1 | Craig Schwartz: a failure in life, a failure in his marriage, and an unacknowledged but brilliant puppeteer. Through the mysterious Dr Lester he takes a job as a filing clerk, where he is instantly drawn to a dazzling co-worker, Maxine (the very opposite of his dowdy wife). He becomes fixated, building a puppet version of her to simulate sex. |
| 2 | He declares his love; Maxine spurns him. Craig then discovers a small door behind a filing cabinet: a portal into the head of John Malkovich, seeing and feeling as the actor does for fifteen minutes before being ejected beside the New Jersey Turnpike. He reveals the portal to Maxine, who proposes selling access at 200 dollars a turn (the business plan), and to his wife Lotte, who tries it and becomes obsessed; it changes her life. |
| 3 | The tour business launches. Lotte, drunk on the experience, announces she is transsexual, meets Maxine and is infatuated. Maxine is attracted to Malkovich himself, so a plan forms: Lotte, inside John's head, will sleep with Maxine, and exactly halfway through the film, at minute 54, intercourse takes place (**MIDPOINT**). Furious, jealous Craig (the only one gaining nothing) kidnaps Lotte, cages her, enters Malkovich himself and takes her place with Maxine. Malkovich grows suspicious as Craig starts puppeteering him from inside, breaks into Lester's building, discovers the scam, enters the portal into his own head, and at the Turnpike demands the portal be closed. |
| 4 | Lotte escapes the cage to warn Maxine, who ironically has found sex with Craig-as-John rather exciting. Lotte visits Dr Lester, who delivers the long "monologuing" beat: the portal exists, and it grants eternal youth; he and his gang of elderly conspirators must occupy Malkovich's head on his forty-fourth birthday to live on. Craig meanwhile grows in confidence and, realizing his puppet skills are the perfect gift, decides to inhabit Malkovich full-time and make him a world-famous puppeteer, with Maxine at his side. Lester offers Lotte a permanent place inside Malkovich; she replies that there is something they should know (**WORST POINT** for Craig). |
| 5 | Craig, as Malkovich, launches a hugely successful puppet career and marries Maxine, who becomes pregnant; the relationship sours. Lester's gang, tipped off by Lotte, kidnap Maxine to force Craig out. In the showdown Maxine and Lotte fall through Malkovich's head together and Lotte learns that she (as Malkovich), not Craig, fathered Maxine's child. Craig, in a moment of remorse, vacates Malkovich; the old people enter; Lotte ends up with Maxine. Jealous, Craig dives back toward the portal, but it has shifted. Seven years later we meet Maxine and Lotte's daughter Emily, and seeing through her eyes we realize Craig is imprisoned inside her, powerless, for the next forty years. |

**The tragic riff:** the worst point here is a moment where Craig is unaware of the forces ranged against him. It could be read as a regression (he only ever wanted to be a puppeteer), but Yorke identifies it as a common riff on tragic structure: the worst point is inverted to become the classic highpoint of the protagonist's ambition. He gets exactly what he wanted, at the moment his doom is sealed.

### Roadmap of change applied to Being John Malkovich

| Act | Stage | Craig |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No knowledge | Craig knows nothing about anything outside his own world |
| 1 | Growing knowledge | Gets the job and starts to become aware |
| 1 | Awakening | Discovers the portal to a whole new world |
| 2 | Doubt | Enters, but is unsure what he has found |
| 2 | Overcoming reluctance | Tells Lotte, who insists on trying the portal herself |
| 2 | Acceptance | Goes into business with Maxine |
| 3 | Experimenting with knowledge | Lotte convinces Maxine to have sex with her inside Malkovich |
| 3 | **BIG CHANGE (midpoint): KNOWLEDGE** | They have sex; Craig's fury erupts |
| 3 | Experimenting post-knowledge | Craig kidnaps Lotte, climbs inside Malkovich, and has sex with Maxine himself |
| 4 | Doubt | Starts to doubt the monster he has become |
| 4 | Growing reluctance | But it is all fraudulent; he begins to overwhelm Malkovich |
| 4 | Regression | Hatches the plan (full-time possession) that will be his downfall |
| 5 | Reawakening | As Maxine and Lotte fight inside Malkovich, Craig doubts... |
| 5 | Re-acceptance | ...before recommitting to his path of domination |
| 5 | Total mastery | Achieves total domination of another person (just not the one he expected) |

## Appendix IV: My Zinc Bed, the structural form

Yorke's framing: David Hare's study of addiction is built entirely on the reconciliation of opposites (passion and repression, feeling and intensity, living and surviving). With a line reaching back to Sophocles' Antigone, the juxtaposition of two polarized views and their eventual assimilation is not just the form of the play; it is its content too. In its story of a reformed alcoholic employed by a bon viveur, each fighting over a love interest, it is uncannily archetypal. One character even quotes the central dialectic: Jung's idea that loving another person is an attempt to compensate for a lack in ourselves, and that the search to complete yourself through another can never succeed. When Victor provocatively mixes a margarita in front of the recovering Paul, he describes it as cold as hell in the mouth, then hot as hell going down: "Contradiction, at the very heart of life." That is the theme of the work, and the play stands as a metaphor for story structure itself.

**Form note:** the play is not written in five acts but in two, each of six scenes. To show the underlying shape, Yorke re-presents it in five acts with the original divisions marked alongside, demonstrating that whatever construct is placed on a narrative, the skeleton stays identical. Watch how Paul moves from repression toward passion, then finds understanding somewhere in between: an ambiguous kind of healing.

### Act-by-act synopsis (original scene markers in the right column)

| Act | Events | Original division |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Paul Peplow, recovering alcoholic and poet, arrives at the house of internet millionaire Victor Quinn. After a fairly disastrous interview, and against both Paul's expectations and ours, Victor offers him a job as a copywriter. | End of scene 3 |
| 2 | Paul meets Elsa, Victor's wife, herself a recovering cocaine addict. At the end of a passionate argument over whether Alcoholics Anonymous is a cult (whether it saved Paul or manufactured the excuse that broke his last relationship), and against both their expectations, they kiss. | End of scene 4 |
| 3 | Paul fully enters Victor's world and starts to live again: fear, terror, excitement. Victor is his opposite, unafraid of drink, dismissive of the "one drink" maxim, mixing margaritas in front of him. Intoxicated by the danger, Paul takes his first drink, indeed demands it, the drink symbolically fused with love (**MIDPOINT**; in the original text this is the END OF PART ONE, end of scene 6). Victor goes away; a drunken Paul turns up at the house and tells Elsa that if he knew he had her he would renounce drink for ever. He pours his whisky into a flowerpot. | End of scene 6 = midpoint; declaration at end of scene 8 |
| 4 | Paul suspects Victor knows about the affair. Summer is ending; Victor and Elsa have rowed (he has accused her of drinking too much); his business is in trouble. Paul tells us he is back in AA, quitting the job, and leaving. | Halfway through scene 9 |
| 5 | Elsa begs him to stay and says she loves him; he answers that loving her would just replace one addiction with another. The sky darkens as Victor enters; it is hinted he knows about the affair. Despite Elsa's pleading, Paul resolves to move on. We learn Victor later dies drunk in a car crash. Paul still attends AA meetings, but having recovered his confidence, his judgement, and perhaps the ability to love, there is a suitably ambiguous suggestion that he has learned to live again. | End of play |

### Roadmap of change applied to My Zinc Bed

| Act | Stage | Paul |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No knowledge | Paul enters Victor's house |
| 1 | Growing knowledge | Starts to learn about Victor |
| 1 | Awakening | Takes the job Victor offers |
| 2 | Doubt | Talks about his old self |
| 2 | Overcoming reluctance | Starts to see the flaws in his old self |
| 2 | Acceptance | Kisses Elsa for the first time |
| 3 | Experimenting with knowledge | Admits being scared; grows more attracted to Elsa |
| 3 | **BIG CHANGE (midpoint): KNOWLEDGE** | Paul drinks, the drink set up symbolically as a declaration of love; he feels the force of "life" |
| 3 | Experimenting post-knowledge | The overwhelming excitement of life, drunk; renounces alcohol for Elsa |
| 4 | Doubt | Retreats from the affair as Victor grows suspicious |
| 4 | Growing reluctance | Sees the dark side of Victor and Elsa's dependency |
| 4 | Regression | Announces he is back in meetings and leaving |
| 5 | Reawakening | Counters Elsa's pleas: loving her is just another addiction |
| 5 | Re-acceptance | Hands in his notice to Victor; no longer dependent |
| 5 | Total mastery | Paul leaves |

## Appendix V: The Godfather, the structural form

Yorke's framing: The Godfather runs like the print in a stick of rock through the whole book, so perfect is its structural form. It is the classic example of a modern tragedy (a hero spiritually, if not physically, dead) and follows the change paradigm to the letter in its tragic form: an archetypal **dark inversion**. Whether it was written in five acts is academic; the template reveals the underlying "into the woods" shape with unnerving clarity. At the midpoint, when Michael points his gun at Sollozzo and McCluskey, he faces the absolute opposite of everything he previously believed in; staring into the woods he pulls the trigger, the opposites become one, and a new character is born. Every subsequent kill destroys another better part of himself.

### Act-by-act synopsis

| Act | Events |
|---|---|
| 1 | Michael Corleone attends his sister Connie's wedding to Carlo with his fiancee Kay. Explaining the family business, he tells her: "That's my family, Kay. Not me." Christmas shopping in New York, he learns his father, Don Corleone, has been shot. |
| 2 | Older brother Sonny takes charge while Michael returns to the family to care for his father. Visiting the hospital, he thwarts a second assassination attempt, and realizes he finds the experience not terrifying but exhilarating. The corrupt police captain McCluskey, frustrated, breaks Michael's jaw. |
| 3 | Michael conceives the plan to murder McCluskey and Sollozzo, the drug baron behind the attack on his father, and executes them both (**MIDPOINT**). He flees to Sicily, falls in love and marries; his wife is murdered by those seeking revenge. In New York, Sonny is murdered too. Don Corleone forswears vengeance, recognizing Don Barzini's power, and secures assurances that Michael can return home safely. |
| 4 | Michael returns to New York and to Kay, promising to make the family legitimate within five years. Pressure builds from the other New York families; his plan to expand into Nevada is ridiculed by casino boss Moe Greene, who derides the Corleones as dinosaurs, and younger brother Fredo falls under Greene's spell. Don Corleone dies peacefully in his garden, but not before warning Michael that there is a traitor in their midst who will reveal himself by offering to broker a deal (**WORST POINT**: alone, surrounded, father gone). |
| 5 | At the funeral, family stalwart Tessio approaches Michael offering to broker a deal with Barzini: the traitor revealed. On the day of the christening of Connie's son, as Michael stands godfather, he strikes: Tessio, Barzini, Greene, the other New York Dons, and Carlo (who he knows set up Sonny's death) are all murdered. Kay asks Michael whether he had Connie's husband killed; he looks her in the eye and denies it. |

```
THE GODFATHER: five acts, dark inversion

   ACT 1        ACT 2        ACT 3        ACT 4        ACT 5
[===========|===========|====[MP]===|======[WP]=|=========*==]
  wedding     hospital     the double   the Don's    baptism
  Don shot    "exhilarated" murder      death        of blood

  [MP] MIDPOINT   : Michael kills Sollozzo and McCluskey; the opposites fuse,
                    a new (dark) character is born
  [WP] WORST POINT: the Don dies after warning of the traitor; Michael alone
   *   CLIMAX     : christening massacre; total (dark) mastery; the lie to Kay
```

### Roadmap of change applied to The Godfather

| Act | Stage | Michael |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No knowledge | Michael stands outside the family firm |
| 1 | Growing knowledge | Hears of the assassination attempt on his father |
| 1 | Awakening | Rushes to the family's side |
| 2 | Doubt | Allows Sonny to take charge |
| 2 | Overcoming reluctance | Formulates his own revenge plan |
| 2 | Acceptance | The plan is accepted by the others |
| 3 | Experimenting with knowledge | The plan is put into operation |
| 3 | **BIG CHANGE (midpoint): KNOWLEDGE** | Michael kills Sollozzo and McCluskey |
| 3 | Experimenting post-knowledge | Flees to Sicily; his wife is killed |
| 4 | Doubt | Returns to New York; promises to go legitimate |
| 4 | Growing reluctance | Pressure builds from the other families and Greene |
| 4 | Regression | His father dies after warning of a traitor in their midst |
| 5 | Reawakening | Learns Tessio is the traitor |
| 5 | Re-acceptance | Kills everyone |
| 5 | Total mastery | Head of the family. Lies to Kay |

The roadmap is identical in shape to Hamlet's; only the polarity differs. In a dark inversion, "total mastery" means the protagonist has fully mastered evil: the flaw has consumed the man.

## Appendix VI: First and last act parallels (the mirror principle)

The beats of act one are echoed, inverted, in act five, measuring how far the protagonist has travelled. The pattern is chiastic (A-B-C answered by C-B-A): the opening beat of act one rhymes with the closing beat of act five, the second beat with the second-from-last, and so on. When structuring, write these two columns first; if they do not answer each other, the story does not yet know what it is about.

### The King's Speech

| Act 1 | Act 5 |
|---|---|
| 1. Makes dreadful speech | 1. Seeks out Logue |
| 2. Refuses to talk to anyone | 2. Insists he has a voice |
| 3. Rejects Logue | 3. Makes brilliant speech |

### The Fabulous Baker Boys

| Act 1 | Act 5 |
|---|---|
| 1. "Will I see you again?" "No" | 1. Meets Monica, turns her down |
| 2. Cool facade; loathes being with brother | 2. Throws facade aside and embraces brother |
| 3. Meets Suzy, wants to pursue her | 3. "Will I see you again?" "Maybe?" |

### The Social Network (from the main text)

| Act 1 | Act 5 |
|---|---|
| 1. Mark dumped by Erica | 1. Winklevoss twins sue Mark |
| 2. Works on Facemash, enlists Eduardo | 2. Works on Facebook, sacks Eduardo |
| 3. Winklevoss twins invite Mark in | 3. Tries to make "friends" with Erica |

Perfect chiasmus: the girl who opens the film closes it; the friend gained in beat two is lost in the mirrored beat; the twins' invitation is answered by their lawsuit.

### The Godfather (from the main text)

| Act 1 | Act 5 |
|---|---|
| 1. A family sacrament: Connie's wedding; the Don receives petitioners and dispenses favours | 1. A family sacrament: the christening; Michael dispenses death |
| 2. Michael the war hero, the legitimate son, outside the business | 2. Michael the Don, the business incarnate |
| 3. "That's my family, Kay. Not me" (the truth) | 3. "Did you kill Carlo?" "No" (the lie to Kay) |

The film opens and closes on ritual celebrations of family; honesty to Kay is mirrored by the lie; the outsider's disclaimer becomes the insider's mask as the door closes on her.

### Thelma and Louise (from the main text)

| Act 1 | Act 5 |
|---|---|
| 1. Both women trapped: Thelma asking her husband's permission, Louise serving tables | 1. Both women free, answering to no one |
| 2. They set out timidly on a sanctioned weekend escape | 2. They commit to flight without return |
| 3. Louise's gunshot: violence forced on them, panic | 3. Their own choice: the leap over the edge, hands joined, taken willingly |

Entrapment mirrored by (fatal) freedom: the accidental transgression of act one becomes the deliberate, self-authored act of act five. Yorke notes the pair also work as protagonist and antagonist in continual opposition, swapping roles across the story, exactly as Richard II and Bolingbroke do.

### E.T. (from the main text)

| Act 1 | Act 5 |
|---|---|
| 1. The ship departs, leaving E.T. stranded and hunted | 1. The ship returns to carry E.T. home |
| 2. Elliott alone, fatherless, disbelieved by everyone | 2. Elliott believed, surrounded by friends and family |
| 3. Elliott hides E.T.: a secret that isolates him | 3. Elliott releases E.T.: a farewell that completes him |

### Strictly Ballroom (from the main text)

| Act 1 | Act 5 |
|---|---|
| 1. At a championship, Scott dances his own steps and is condemned | 1. At the Pan-Pacifics, Scott dances his own steps and triumphs |
| 2. His partner abandons him; the establishment closes ranks | 2. The establishment sabotages him; Fran and family stand with him |
| 3. He dismisses the beginner Fran | 3. He chooses Fran, and the whole floor joins their dance |

## Appendix VII: A lightning guide to screenwriting gurus

Yorke's framing: if you ever doubted that snake-oil salesmen traversed the screenwriting frontier, type "screenplay structure" into a search engine. There are a great many gurus, some entertaining, some less so; the hallmarks of the worst are grotesque over-complication, bizarre terminology, blind certainty, and ceaseless demands for money in return for "truth". Their main achievement is to give the serious study of structure a bad name. What is fascinating, though, is that if you follow their instructions you may very well learn to write a good script, because fundamentally all of them are trying to articulate the same thing. There is no greater proof of an archetype than the realization that all these separate theories (Yorke's own included) are really identical. Propp and Campbell would never have called themselves gurus, but their inclusion underlines the argument for a unifying structure. All the models fit, some more readily than others; the comparison is deliberately simplistic, and the originals reward reading, but the point stands: all are grasping to capture the true shape of story.

### Aristotle (Poetics, c. 335 BC)

- **Core model:** a whole with a beginning, middle and end; unity of action; complication and unravelling; peripeteia (reversal) and anagnorisis (recognition) producing catharsis.
- **Five-act mapping:** beginning = act 1; middle = acts 2-4, with recognition sitting naturally at the midpoint; unravelling and end = act 5.

### Horace and Terence (Roman drama)

- **Core model:** Horace's Ars Poetica prescribes that a play be neither shorter nor longer than five acts; Terence's comedies model the practice. The direct ancestor of the five-act template later formalized in Elizabethan printing (whether Shakespeare consciously "thought in acts" is contested, but the five-movement principle is visible in the work).
- **Five-act mapping:** the template itself.

### Gustav Freytag (Technique of the Drama, 1863)

- **Core model:** the pyramid: introduction, rising action, CLIMAX AT THE APEX, falling action, catastrophe; between the parts stand binding "moments", including the "exciting force" that stirs the action after the introduction. Yorke concedes that Freytag's paragraph on the exciting force neatly encapsulates the premise of his own book.
- **Five-act mapping:** exciting force = the inciting incident, late in act 1; the apex placed at the CENTER = the midpoint (Freytag is the great witness that the "climax" of classical analysis is the middle, not the end); catastrophe = act 5.

### Vladimir Propp (Morphology of the Folktale, 1928)

- **Core model:** 31 functions of the Russian folktale occurring in fixed sequence, distributed among seven character roles. Not a guru: a morphologist whose data happen to prove the archetype.
- **Five-act mapping:** absentation, interdiction, villainy, departure = acts 1-2; struggle, branding, victory = act 3; return and pursuit = act 4; recognition, exposure of the false hero, transfiguration and wedding = act 5.

### Joseph Campbell (The Hero with a Thousand Faces, 1949)

- **Core model:** the monomyth: separation, initiation, return; the hero passes through pairs of opposites, discovers and assimilates his opposite ("his own unsuspected self") and returns with the boon.
- **Five-act mapping:** separation (call, refusal, threshold) = acts 1-2; supreme ordeal = the act 3 midpoint; return (magic flight, crossing the return threshold) = act 4; master of two worlds = act 5.
- **Yorke's caveat:** the monomyth is real but is not the source of structure; it is a macrocosm of scene structure. Myth did not give birth to structure; structure gave birth to myth. The "nuclear unit of the monomyth" is really just a scene: thesis meets antithesis and each assimilates the other.

### Christopher Vogler (The Writer's Journey, 1992)

- **Core model:** Campbell popularized for Hollywood in 12 stages, from ordinary world through call, refusal, mentor, threshold, tests, ordeal, reward, road back, resurrection, return with elixir.
- **Five-act mapping:** ordinary world through first threshold = acts 1-2; tests, approach, ordeal and reward = act 3 (ordeal at the midpoint); road back = act 4; resurrection and return = act 5. Substitute the cure for the character's flaw for the "elixir" and the model unlocks completely.
- **Yorke's caveats:** the claim that every first act contains a refusal of the call does not hold universally; the "midpoint as a moment of death" argument rests on Psycho and on E.T. (who does not in fact die there); and the mystical terminology reads like the back of a 1960s Grateful Dead sleeve. Useful map, cultish packaging.

### Robert McKee (Story, 1999)

- **Core model:** inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax, resolution; the gap between expectation and result; the controlling idea. McKee gave the term "inciting incident" (coined by Thomas Baldwin in 1945, used once by Syd Field) its modern currency.
- **Five-act mapping:** inciting incident closes act 1; progressive complications = acts 2-4; crisis = the act 4 worst point; climax and resolution = act 5.
- **Yorke's caveat:** a tendency to rebaptize simple basic tenets (such as theme) with new, more complex and entirely unnecessary terminology.

### Syd Field (Screenplay, 1979)

- **Core model:** the three-act paradigm: setup, confrontation, resolution, hinged on Plot Point I and Plot Point II, with a midpoint and two "pinch points" added in later work.
- **Five-act mapping:** Plot Point I = the act 1/act 2 turn (acceptance of the journey); Field's midpoint = the midpoint; the pinch points are, in truth, act breaks (which is why his "second act" is really three acts); Plot Point II = the act 4 crisis; act III = act 5.

### Blake Snyder (Save the Cat!, 2005)

- **Core model:** the 15-beat sheet: opening image, theme stated, catalyst, debate, break into two, B story, fun and games, midpoint, bad guys close in, all is lost, dark night of the soul, break into three, finale, final image.
- **Five-act mapping:** catalyst and debate = acts 1-2; midpoint = midpoint; all is lost plus dark night of the soul = the act 4 worst point; break into three and finale = act 5.
- **Yorke's caveat:** the page-number dogmatism ("Put it where it belongs: page 12... Do it") is the blind certainty of an upside-down world; the beats themselves are the archetype under a marketing layer.

### John Truby (The Anatomy of Story, 2007)

- **Core model:** 22 steps built on 7 key steps: weakness and need, desire, opponent, plan, battle, self-revelation, new equilibrium.
- **Five-act mapping:** weakness/need and desire = act 1; opponent and plan = act 2; escalating battle = acts 3-4; self-revelation and new equilibrium = act 5.
- **Yorke's caveat:** endless unnecessary complication; but the seven key steps are the five-act engine renamed.

### Christopher Booker (The Seven Basic Plots, 2004)

- **Core model:** seven recurring plots, all running on a five-stage meta-pattern: anticipation, dream, frustration, nightmare, resolution (or, in tragedy, destruction).
- **Five-act mapping:** the cleanest one-to-one match of any system: anticipation = act 1, dream = act 2, frustration = act 3, nightmare = act 4, resolution = act 5.
- **Yorke's caveat:** a frustrating tome; in his rush to impose a unifying pattern Booker ignores the crucial elements that do not seem to fit, above all the midpoint, and though he observes his pattern matching Macbeth's act structure he never pursues the relationship to act structure as a whole. Many valid, some brilliant, observations all the same.

### Lajos Egri (The Art of Dramatic Writing, 1942)

- **Core model:** everything grows from a clearly stated premise proven by the action; three-dimensional character; orchestration of opposites; unity of opposites making conflict unbreakable until transformation.
- **Five-act mapping:** premise asserted through character flaw = act 1; the unity of opposites locks protagonist and antagonist through acts 2-4; the premise is proven in act 5.
- **Yorke's caveat:** the nearest thing to a true antecedent of the dialectical view (and Yorke elsewhere calls the book more poetry than "truth"), but Egri insists one side should win, which negates the idea of synthesis.

### Maureen Murdock (The Heroine's Journey, 1990)

- **Core model:** a corrective to Campbell for female protagonists: separation from the feminine, road of trials and the boon of success, spiritual aridity, descent to the goddess, reconnection with the feminine, healing the mother/daughter split, integration of masculine and feminine.
- **Five-act mapping:** separation and trials = acts 1-2; the illusory boon and the descent = midpoint into act 4 (aridity as the worst point); reconnection and integration = act 5. The same journey to assimilate the missing opposite, gendered.

### Frank Daniel (the eight-sequence structure)

- **Core model:** a screenwriting teacher (Columbia, USC) who divided the feature into eight sequences of roughly 12-15 minutes, each a mini-movie with its own protagonist goal, tension, climax and resolution (documented in Paul Joseph Gulino's Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach, 2004).
- **Five-act mapping:** sequences 1-2 = acts 1-2 (status quo, inciting incident, lock-in); sequences 3-4 close at the midpoint; sequences 5-6 = the decline to the act 4 crisis; sequences 7-8 = climax and resolution, act 5. The fractal principle made pedagogy: each sequence is itself a five-act story in miniature.

## The master key

Every model above reduces to the same engine. A protagonist leaves home (thesis), journeys into the woods to confront an opposite (antithesis), touches the central truth at the exact middle of the story, pays for that knowledge at the worst point, and returns home changed (synthesis). Act structure, the roadmap of change, the hero's journey, the beat sheet, the sequence method: all are fractal enlargements of a single scene, in which a character meets their opposite and assimilates it. When mapping any new story, find the midpoint truth and the first/last act mirror before anything else; the rest of the shape follows as water finds its level.
