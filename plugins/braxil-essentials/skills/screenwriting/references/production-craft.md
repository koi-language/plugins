# Production Craft: Shooting for the Edit, Coverage, Planning

Source: The Filmmaker's Handbook (Ascher and Pincus, 4th ed.), chapter 9, "Shooting the Movie". What a writer or storyboard author must know about how footage actually gets shot and cut, so the plan on paper survives production. Pair with the visual-grammar reference for shot language.

## Shoot for the edit

- Production is not an end in itself; it is a means to an end. Everything done in production exists to ensure that the editing room has the elements needed to tell the story.
- The director constantly thinks not just about what the camera is capturing, but about how that footage will be edited: how the scene being filmed will translate to the screen and integrate with scenes already shot and scenes not yet shot. Editing experience is extremely useful for directors and cinematographers.
- This does NOT mean pre-editing the movie rigidly. Hopefully the director provides the editor with material that can go together in multiple ways. Even tightly scripted films are often transformed in pace, point of view, and storytelling in the editing room; anticipate the editor's needs.
- Both the order of scenes in the original script and the overall length of the movie are often changed substantially in editing. Do not paint yourself into a corner: plan coverage so shots and scenes can be put together in more than one way.
- In documentary this is trickier still: you capture events whose meaning and place in the film you may not know yet, which calls for even more flexibility.
- The methods cross over constantly: documentary and fiction filmmakers should be versed in each other's techniques.

## Coverage doctrine

While planning or shooting, constantly ask how the shot being taken might work with the shots already gotten and the shots still needed. The standing checklist:

- Do you have an establishing shot?
- Do you have cutaways?
- Have you got interesting close-ups?
- Do you have reactions?
- Have you provided enough options for editing, and does anything lock the scene into a single assembly?

Rules and reasons:

- Coverage means options. Multiple angles in the editing room let you change the pace of the scene, direct audience attention to different aspects, use the best performances, and edit around camera or acting errors. If a scene is covered with only one or two angles or takes, options are limited: "many an editor has lamented a director's lack of coverage."
- **Master caution**: filming a continuous master of an entire scene is time-consuming on set (everything must go right at once), and even if you plan to play the scene as a single shot, surprise errors show up in the editing room and you will want to cut around them. A long take can be beautiful, but if the sequence must be shortened, your beautiful three-minute shot becomes a burden.
- **Editing insurance**: shooting a reaction shot or a cutaway is cheap; do it even if you do not intend to use it.
- **Reactions**: directors tend to concentrate on whoever is talking. Some scenes are more interesting for the reactions of the other characters.
- **Off-camera performance**: when filming a close-up of one actor talking to an off-screen actor, mic the off-camera actor too; those takes are sometimes better than the on-camera ones. Higher-budget films run two cameras in this situation. For very wide shots where no boom can get close, record the dialogue a few times wild (sound, no picture) with the mic in close: far cheaper than ADR.
- **Blocking is choreography**: blocking the camera and the actors together, keep the image as dynamic as possible; be attentive to the depth of the space, either to show it or to let actors move through it.
- **Two coverage strategies**: (1) full redundancy: shoot the whole scene in wide shot, then reshoot the whole scene again in close-up (maximum flexibility in editing, maximum time and footage); (2) pre-editing: decide in advance to film only the opening and closing of the scene wide and the bulk of the dialogue in close-up (saves shoot time and footage, narrows editing options). Hitchcock pre-edited so precisely that editor George Tomasini joked that cutting a scene sometimes meant trimming off the slates and stringing the shots together.

## The shooting ratio

The ratio of total footage filmed to final running time. It varies by type of movie, budget, and directorial style.

| Production type | Typical ratio |
|---|---|
| Carefully planned drama | About 5:1 to 15:1 |
| Reality TV, multiple cameras | Up to 450 hours for a one-hour program |

What drives it on a drama: script length, number of scenes, number of setups per scene, takes per setup, and the coverage strategy chosen (full redundancy versus pre-editing).

Tradeoffs:

- **High ratio buys**: a more relaxed shoot, bigger risks, letting actors play with a scene, not stopping between takes; in unpredictable documentary, capturing things you would otherwise miss.
- **High ratio costs**: shots become relaxed and rambling, with no particular beginning or end, and may be impossible to cut; extra production time, stock, storage, and processing; many days just to view and log the material.
- **Film versus digital discipline**: film is expensive, so shooting film forces you to edit in your head and make every foot count; with digital it is almost harder to turn the camera off than to keep shooting. Students who learn on film shoot more carefully and thoughtfully than those who start on digital. Emulate the film discipline even on digital.
- No matter how predictable you assume the action will be, the unexpected happens: weather changes, flubbed lines, technical problems. Additional takes are invariably needed in acted work, and documentary is always unpredictable. Budget ratio for it.

## How many takes?

- **The Lumet school**: rehearse actors thoroughly before the shoot and film only a few takes, because the first takes have the freshest performances. **The Kubrick school**: shoot numerous takes, grooming each shot to perfection. The saying: the best takes are the first and the tenth (spontaneity versus practice), but the budget may not permit ten.
- Inexperienced directors tend to shoot more takes and mark more of them as preferred.
- **Always shoot at least two keepers** of any shot, as a safety in case one is damaged or has unnoticed technical problems.
- **The variation rule**: even after a good take, try it again faster, or slower, or with something varied in the reading or action. In the editing room you will wish for more OPTIONS to choose from, not more copies of the same reading and blocking.
- **Busted takes**: when something goes wrong mid-take, reset quickly ("back to one") without a lot of chatter, keeping momentum and concentration. Some directors roll straight into a second or third take without stopping to reslate; it helps actors but can confuse the editing room.
- **Circle takes**: after each take the director tells the script supervisor or logger whether the take is good (a circle take) plus any notes. Digital post usually has every take available, but there must be a record of which ones the director liked.

## Reviewing footage, dailies, pickups

- Playing back every good take on set slows production badly; but DO check the best takes before breaking down a camera or lighting setup and moving on.
- **Dailies**: the director, DP, and others evaluate footage as it gets shot, preferably on a large screen. Uncut dailies do not look like polished movies; they are repetitive, rough, and messy, and it takes experience to see the potential in raw footage. Some directors invite actors to dailies; others prefer actors not see themselves and get self-conscious.
- On some productions the editor cuts scenes as they are shot: strong feedback for the director; you know things are working, or you see what needs adjusting or reshooting.
- **Pickups and reshoots**: errors found in dailies or editing require going back for additional shots to fill a sequence (a cutaway from a car window, a missing reaction shot), generally with a reduced crew. To match later: take stills of sets, lighting setups, makeup, and costumes, and keep detailed notes on lenses and camera angles.
- Anticipate reshoots, pickups, and ADR by building them into the schedule and the actors' contracts.

## Directing actors (what generalizes to written scenes)

### Casting

- Casting is decisive. Huston to Michael Caine: "The art of direction, Michael, is casting. If you've casted right, you don't have to say anything." Good casting makes the director's job enormously easier; bad casting may sink a great script.
- Actors must be right for their roles AND work as an ensemble. Prepare a casting breakdown (every role with a short description). Prepare **sides** (excerpted script portions per character) and get them to actors before the audition; schedule callbacks in different combinations.
- Record every audition. Ask actors to try lines a few different ways: how they take direction matters. Watch them listening and performing action without dialogue; a major part of acting is nonverbal. Beware headshots, which mislead (except for extras, where they are all you have).
- Union actors are generally more experienced and expensive, though they may reduce or defer salaries for low-budget productions; known stars can unlock financing and marketing, and many will appear in a low-budget film if the script is good and the days required are few. Open auditions surface a few undiscovered gems and a lot of inexperience.

### Rehearsal

- A table read (or a read with movement) lets you hear the dialogue and get ideas for direction; reading with the actual cast builds relationships.
- Philosophies differ: Mike Leigh uses rehearsal to let actors shape story and dialogue; other directors want the material done fresh on the shoot with little prep.
- Rehearsal serves the actors AND the crew: blocking affects lighting and camerawork and vice versa. Do not make actors stand around while lighting is set (that is what stand-ins are for); marks are "spiked" with tape on the floor. Once lighting, props, and dolly track are set, flexibility to change things is limited.

### Protecting performance

- Actors are extremely vulnerable to disruptions of mood; treat them with respect and deference. Only the director gives performance instructions; anyone else routes through the director.
- In intimate or difficult scenes, some actors prefer that crew members not even make eye contact while the camera rolls; sometimes clear the set of unneeded crew.
- The director's tone of voice, even in calling "action", sets the mood of the take. No shouting or arguments in front of actors; do not involve them in technical business; give them a comfortable space off set.
- The director should wear a wireless headphone feed from sound to hear the dialogue as actually recorded.
- **Video village trap**: directors who bury themselves at the monitors leave actors feeling isolated, and a crowd kibitzing over the monitor creates a too-many-cooks problem.

### Reading to camera

- When a host or correspondent reads to camera, the eye line must be as close to the lens as possible or they will appear to be reading. Solutions, cheapest first: cue cards with a hole cut for the lens; a teleprompter mounted in front of the lens; an ear prompter (earwig) playing back their own recorded lines while they speak (needs practice; never first tried on the shoot day).

## Wardrobe, makeup, and set rules the camera cares about

- Avoid very bright or very dark clothing. White shirts burn out (overexpose) when the camera is exposed for proper skin tones, especially in daylight; pastel or off-white shades work better.
- Avoid fine patterns like checks and stripes: moire. Avoid shiny surfaces and jewelry (dulling spray or a little dry soap kills highlights; lights can be flagged).
- Glasses: light people with glasses from high above or from the side to avoid kicks in the lenses.
- These clothing guidelines apply equally to wall treatments, furniture, and set dressing.
- Facial shine from sweating under lights is fixed with translucent face powder (applied to the brush, not the skin); touch up whenever you see shine.

## Script preparation

- Narrative films typically move story, then treatment, then screenplay in standard page format (industry expects it).
- In a script meant to be read by funders or actors, keep camera direction and blocking to a minimum: the reader should experience the movie as it plays, not the mechanics.
- Memorable scenes use surprisingly few words: looks, actions, terse exchanges beat long expository dialogue. Scenes almost always play better in the edit with much less dialogue than was written, partly for pacing, partly because audiences prefer to make connections themselves. **Read every line of dialogue aloud before production**, ideally with the actors. There is no better time to trim dialogue and whole scenes than before the shoot.
- **One script page equals about one minute of screen time** in standard layout. Dialogue scenes are more predictable than action scenes; repartee runs fast and slow-paced delivery runs long, so verify by speaking the lines with a stopwatch.
- Vet the screenplay for legal issues before production (real names at real addresses, music that needs clearing).

### The shooting script

- Approaching production, a shooting script is prepared: specific camera angles, more action detail.
- Every scene is numbered, and all scene and page numbers are **locked**: if page 18 grows, the extra page becomes 18A; a new scene added after scene 20 becomes 20A. Revision pages are dated and printed on colored paper: first revision blue, second pink, then yellow, and so on.

## Previsualization ladder (simplest tool first)

| Tool | What it is | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Table read / rehearsal | Hearing the script aloud, with or without movement | Trimming dialogue, forming relationships, direction ideas |
| Floor plans | Overhead diagrams of camera angles and blocking | The cheapest way to plan coverage of a scene |
| Lens sketches | Simple drawings of what would be seen through the lens | Individual key frames of a sequence |
| Director's finder | Handheld viewer (or phone app) showing a scene at different focal lengths | Scouting locations and choosing lenses |
| Storyboards | Shot-by-shot drawings of how action and camerawork play on screen | Effects shots and complex setups where many people must make the shot work |
| Storyboard / previz software | Board apps, and 3D apps that simulate a camera moving through space | When drawing skill is short; planning set construction or CGI shots |
| Video rough draft | Shoot a cheap video draft of scenes and edit it before production (Coppola and others) | Testing angles, moves, dialogue, blocking; discovering, with time to react, that the plan should change |

- Some directors previsualize meticulously: Hitchcock worked out the entire film beforehand and claimed that shooting was an uneventful execution of the movie he had already seen in his mind. Others find detailed boards limiting. Expect many changes between the storyboard and what is actually shot and edited.
- A film shoot is not an environment for fresh thinking: pausing to ponder puts you behind schedule while a crowd waits. Decide as much as possible before the day.

## Script breakdown and the stripboard

- Every scene in the script is broken down on a **breakdown sheet** listing the people and resources it requires: cast (principals and extras), crew, stunts, props, wardrobe, makeup/hair, vehicles, special effects and equipment, music.
- Scene length is measured in **one-eighth-page increments** (a half-page scene is four-eighths).
- The **production board (stripboard)** holds a strip per scene, color coded interior/exterior and day/night; strips are moved around to form and revise the schedule (physical board or scheduling software).
- A **day-out-of-days** report shows every day each actor or element is scheduled, revealing whether rearranging lets you film an actor's scenes back to back.
- The director should have a **shot list for each day**, discussed in advance with the whole production team (do not assume people know the plan without confirming), with backups in case equipment dies, weather turns, or someone fails to show.

## Scheduling wisdom

### Preparation mindset

- Shooting a movie is almost always chaotic: countless simultaneous decisions and events out of your control. Preparation turns it into CONTROLLED chaos that still delivers the footage on schedule and budget. "Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity."
- For the director, preparation means previsualizing action and camerawork; for the producer, hiring a good team and having resources ready on time, and knowing which wish-list items can be cut; for the DP, having the equipment, knowing it, and being confident it works, with visual style and key shots worked out with the director.
- Some shoots can be planned to the nubs; others must be improvised in the moment. Prepare for both.
- Equipment: assemble and test the whole package before the shoot; charge and refresh batteries the night before; keep gear organized so anything is in hand within seconds; buy expendables in advance; when traveling, hand-carry the camera and delicate lenses.

### The rules

| Rule | Reason |
|---|---|
| Shoot by location, not script order | Returning to the same place to set up multiple times is too inefficient |
| "Shoot out" actors with limited availability | Group all of an actor's scenes together |
| Wides of crowd scenes first, close-ups after | Dismiss most of the crowd early |
| Easy scenes first, complex later; never key scenes on day one | Crews go through an arc: bumpy start, then a groove, then exhaustion-ragged |
| Key emotional scenes late in the schedule | A relationship story plays more naturally when the actors know each other; intense scenes benefit from the shoot's accumulated trust |
| Exteriors relatively early, and always keep an interior cover set ready | If weather kills the exterior, the day is not lost |
| Watch the light window, not just the location | Midday light is flat and harsh; early and late light casts interesting shadows; magic hour shows lights plus landscape; thin cloud enhances color and softens shadows (kind to close-ups); haze obscures distant shots |
| Duplicate wardrobe and props | A lost prop or a dirty shirt should not stop production |
| Avoid company moves within a day | Moving locations invariably burns hours |
| Meals every six hours, craft service always available | Hungry crews slow down |
| Turnaround (end of one day to start of the next) at least twelve hours | Exhaustion has caused serious accidents; night shoots make this acute |
| Low-budget pace: about three to four pages per twelve-hour day | Dialogue shoots faster than action; many short scenes take longer than a few long ones |
| Expect to pare the setup list | It is normal to enter a day with a full list and cut to essentials as time and light run out |
| Leave schedule room for reshoots, pickups, ADR | Build them into the schedule and the actors' contracts |
| Backtime preproduction three to four months | Casting, hires, equipment, permits, insurance, and especially music licenses take months |

## On-set procedure (condensed)

### Slating protocol

| Step | Call | Who |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Announce the take, "last looks" | AD (everyone finishes prep) |
| 2 | Call for quiet, then "sound" | AD |
| 3 | "Speed" (audio recorder rolling) | Sound recordist |
| 4 | "Camera" | AD |
| 5 | "Speed" / "rolling" | Camera operator |
| 6 | Read scene and take from the slate, "mark(er)", close the sticks | AC |
| 7 | "Action" | Director |
| 8 | Camera and recorder keep rolling until "cut" | Director |

- Slate contents: production company, project, director, DP, scene and take numbers, date; camera letter (A, B, C) if multiple cameras; camera roll / card number; sound take and roll if used.
- **Scene and setup lettering**: "Scene 8" is the first setup of scene 8; "Scene 8A" is the second camera angle; the letters O and I are skipped (they look like numbers). An alternative system numbers slates sequentially across the whole production, incrementing at every new camera angle.
- Slate every drama take even when sync is not needed (visual ID at the head of the take). MOS shots are slated too, with "MOS" written on the slate and the hinged bar NOT raised.
- **Head slates** are standard (fast syncing). **Tail slates** (held upside down, call "tail slate" or "end sticks") suit delicate acted scenes and unstaged documentary, at the cost of slower syncing. If a slate must be redone, announce "second sticks". Slate gently: a crashing clapper rattles actors, and actors should not be rushed to begin immediately after the slate.
- If the slate is in frame on the very first frame, it becomes the clip's thumbnail in editing: free labeling.

### Logs and the lined script

- **Basic take log**, per take: date and location; card/tape/roll number (never two media with the same number); scene number and description; take number; timecode start; whether the take was good, plus performance or content notes. In documentary there is little time to log; write notes at least at the end of every day, indicating what was shot and which media cover what.
- Much of the slate information can now be logged electronically as metadata with the picture and sound files; wireless timecode readouts spare the logger from bugging the operator, and editing systems can import logging data directly.
- **The script supervisor** tracks continuity (gestures, wardrobe, weather, hairdo, prop state, line readings) and confirms everything was shot from the angles called for. They mark the **lined script**: a vertical line down the script pages for each camera setup that covers them; zigzag portions of a line mean the dialogue or action is off camera in that setup. The lined script shows at a glance what coverage exists and what is missing, and tells the editor what was filmed.
- The script supervisor also produces script notes (comments per take, lenses, continuity issues) and an editor's daily log of all takes in shoot order; a feature also files a daily production report each night.
- On film shoots the camera assistant's **camera report** lists every take per roll; circled takes are the good ones (in 35mm, usually only circled takes get printed).

### Key crew (who does what)

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Executive producer | Arranges financing or otherwise develops the production |
| Producer | Raises money, assembles the package (script, director, actors), owns budget, hires and fires |
| Director | Translates the script into visual terms; directs the actors; responsible for the production unit |
| 1st AD | Keeps the shoot on schedule; maintains order on set |
| 2nd AD | Call sheets; makes sure needed actors are present |
| Script supervisor | Continuity; the lined script; editor's notes |
| UPM / line producer | Relations with outside labor and suppliers; keeps production on schedule |
| Production coordinator | Shipping, transport, lodging |
| Location manager / scout | Finds locations, arranges logistics |
| DP (cinematographer) | Composes shots, plans camera movements, designs lighting, with the director |
| Camera operator | Runs the camera during takes on larger units |
| 1st AC | Follow focus; camera equipment; checks the gate |
| 2nd AC / clapper loader | Slate, loading, camera reports |
| DIT | Digital: camera parameters, look, offload and backup of files |
| Gaffer / best boy / electricians | Place and power the lights per the DP |
| Grips / dolly grip | Rigging, moving set pieces; the dolly grip pushes the dolly and needs as much finesse as the operator |
| Sound recordist / boom operator | Records sound; maneuvers the mic |
| Second unit | Stunts, crowd scenes, battles, effects: the scenes shot without sound, with their own director and camera crew |
| PAs | Everything else |

- Crew size is a balancing act: too small and people are overburdened and slow; but crew growth multiplies itself (more people need more cars, meals, lodging, which needs more people), makes each hour more expensive, and adds pressure that kills experimentation. Documentaries want small crews for access and low disruption.
- Every day, an AD issues **call sheets**: when each cast and crew member reports, what will be filmed, contacts and directions. Keep a production book that can answer any question quickly.

### Location scouting checklist (the recce)

| Area | Questions |
|---|---|
| Direction / art direction | Is there space to get the camera back from the action? Room for dolly track? Are walls, furniture, and artwork usable or must they change? Problem views out the windows? Crowd control for exteriors? |
| Lighting | What is the natural light and how will it change over the shoot (sun-position apps help)? How much artificial light is needed? Ceilings high enough to hide lights? Enough electrical power, or generators? |
| Camera | Special lenses needed (wide angle for small rooms)? Can normal supports be used, or handheld/Steadicam? Adjustments for high or low light levels? |
| Sound | Quiet enough? Flight path or highway? Squeaking floors? Too reverberant? Does the noise level change at certain hours? |
| Production / support | When is filming allowed, and what does permission cost? Parking or permits? Enough bathrooms? Staging areas for equipment, wardrobe, makeup? Heat management? Easy to find? Internet? |

- You usually need a location release, sometimes a permit and insurance bond. Filmmakers routinely shoot the exterior of a building in one place and "its" interior somewhere entirely different. A studio (or even a quiet space, a few movable flats, and good art direction) solves most location problems if the budget allows.
- Photograph potential sites from multiple angles; a director's finder helps block out shots at different focal lengths while scouting.

## Documentary styles and the stylistic framework

Documentary is never a plain "document" of reality; all filming and editing involves selection and point of view. Before writing or boarding any nonfiction (or fiction imitating nonfiction), pick the stylistic framework deliberately.

| Style | How it works | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Actuality (Edison, Lumiere) | A camera set up to record actual events in a single continuous shot | The primal form: the world on film as novelty |
| Grierson hybrid | Staged, orchestrated events performed by real people (nonactors), scripted and shot much like a narrative film; authoritative "voice of God" narration spells out the message | Born when equipment was too heavy to chase life |
| Cinema verite / direct cinema | Lightweight sync-sound cameras spontaneously react to events and capture life as lived; often no narration or interviews | Unselfconscious subjects reveal how they live; self-conscious ones reveal themselves in how they perform. Wiseman's films: only sync-sound scenes, no framing devices, scenes playing near real time |
| Personal documentary | The filmmaker explores their own life or openly acknowledges the camera and their role interpreting events; often self-narrated | Embraces subjectivity instead of an illusion of objectivity |
| Magazine / correspondent | A correspondent guides the story: interviews, stand-ups to camera, interview bites edited with B-roll | Journalism roots; producible on a short schedule, like writing a newspaper piece |
| Historical / issue film | Talking heads plus archival material (stock footage), present-day location footage, sometimes stylized reenactments (no dialogue, faces unseen) | The more screen time and dialogue reenactments get, the closer to docudrama |
| Reality TV | People placed in preplanned situations or competitions; unscripted dialogue inside a designed setup | Shot to be cut into an episode format |

- **Script or no script**: historical and issue films begin with research, then a script or detailed treatment; preinterviews establish roughly what people will say, and field footage illustrates ideas already laid out. The opposite pole: the shooting IS the research; you focus on story threads and characters, and the arc emerges in shooting and a long edit. Some stories carry an obvious arc (an election). The risky observed-life approach can yield characters who change, are born, and die: a depth no other method reaches.
- **Framework questions to answer up front**: how will the audience learn about the subject? Watching events unfold verite-style? Interviews with subjects, heard over other footage? Experts? Narration, and if so, a disembodied voice or someone on screen (subject, filmmaker, correspondent)?

### Filming real life

- The more subjects trust you, the more comfortable they are on camera. Spend personal time with the camera off; discuss what kind of film it is, where it will show, what is okay to shoot. Get releases (at the outset or later), but do not grant subjects formal approval over the edit: you are making the movie, not them. Exhibition can seriously affect subjects' lives, positively and negatively; weigh it while shooting and editing.
- Keep the crew small and lights few so shooting stays relaxed and low-key. Keep the camera on the shoulder much of the time so there is no big distinction between shooting and waiting; turn off the tally light; keep slates quiet, use tail slates, or skip them. The point is not to be sneaky but to make filming subtle, with a fluid transition between shooting and not shooting.
- Wireless mics free subjects to roam without a boom in their faces; show them the mute button so they can have privacy.
- Being filmed becomes boring fast; the novelty wears off, and that is exactly what you want. Approaches range from fly-on-the-wall silence to continuous conversation with subjects; pick one and be consistent.

## Documentary principles that generalize

- The verite camera operator must **think like a director and an editor at once** while spontaneously reacting to events: the tendency is to fixate on the central action or the person talking; remind yourself the audience also needs context (wide shot) and reactions from others. Think about the sequence as a whole and ask constantly if you have enough coverage; provide multiple editing options and shots that could make interesting beginnings or endings.
- **Give every shot and move a shape**: start on a static frame held a few seconds, transition into the movement, glide to a stop on another frame and hold it. Constant aimless movement produces uncuttable footage.
- **Shoot so time can be condensed**: a two-hour dinner must play as two minutes. A locked two-shot the whole time would be impossible to cut; get a variety of angles, two-shots and close-ups, and ample footage of the person LISTENING. An OTS from behind the talker hides moving lips and can carry any stretch of the audio; the same logic covers phone calls (let the phone block the lips) and musicians (neutral shots without visible finger positions).
- Cutaways are often essential to making a documentary scene work at all.
- **Interview craft**: decide whether the interviewer's voice will be heard. If not, subjects must incorporate the question into the answer ("I was born in London", not "London"), speak in full sentences, and never say "as I said before" or refer to "you". Set a relaxed tone; explain why you will nod but not respond. Listen constantly for how the material can be edited; stop long unbroken run-ons and ask for the idea more succinctly, or in separate short bits. The first answer is usually the freshest; for a re-ask, change focal length so the first part of one answer can be cut with the second part of the other.
- **Interview camera language**: alternate subjects facing screen left and screen right; give opposing opinions opposing screen directions. A setting can characterize the speaker (a kitchen, an activity, walking, driving); a neutral or limbo backdrop gives consistency across many subjects but goes dull; green screen opens the background but can feel artificial. Some shoot interviews static and change focal length only BETWEEN shots (never cutting mid-zoom); a well-timed slow zoom during emotional material also works if it is paced to the phrases of speech. A dolly on curved track keeps a sense of movement at constant distance. Multiple cameras make condensing without jump cuts easy and avoid the fake reaction shot filmed after the fact (mocked in Broadcast News, where the correspondent's on-camera tears were a retake).
- If lower-thirds will identify speakers, leave room at the bottom of the frame.
- If only the audio of an interview will be used (voice-over that makes the subject narrate the film), recording with an audio recorder alone, or pointing the camera away, can put people more at ease.
- The same discipline applies to any "found footage" or mockumentary format you write: the fiction must obey the physics and grammar of real documentary shooting to be credible.
