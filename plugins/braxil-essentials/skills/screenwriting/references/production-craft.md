# Production Craft: Shooting for the Edit, Coverage, Planning

Source: Filmmaker's Handbook (Ascher & Pincus, 4th ed.), ch. 9. What a writer/storyboarder must know about how footage is shot+cut so the paper plan survives production.

## Shoot for the edit
- Production = a means: give the edit room what it needs to tell the story.
- Direct while thinking how footage cuts + integrates with scenes shot/not-yet-shot; editing experience helps directors/DPs.
- Don't pre-edit rigidly: give material that assembles multiple ways; even scripted films transform in pace/POV/storytelling in edit.
- Scene order + length often change in edit; plan coverage so shots/scenes go together >1 way. Don't paint into a corner.
- Doc needs even more flexibility (meaning/placement unknown while shooting). Fiction + doc techniques cross over; learn both.

## Coverage doctrine
Ask how this shot works with shots got + needed. Checklist: establishing shot? cutaways? interesting CUs? reactions? enough options, and does anything lock the scene into one assembly?
- **Coverage = options**: multiple angles change pace, redirect attention, use best performances, cut around errors. Few angles = few options ("many an editor has lamented a director's lack of coverage").
- **Master caution**: continuous master is slow (all must go right at once); even a planned one-shot scene hides errors you'll cut around; a long take that must shorten becomes a burden.
- **Editing insurance**: reactions/cutaways cheap; shoot even if unplanned.
- **Reactions**: directors fixate on the talker; some scenes live in others' reactions.
- **Off-camera performance**: mic the off-screen actor on a CU (may be better); high budget = two cameras; very wide = record dialogue wild (sound only, mic close) vs costly ADR.
- **Blocking = choreography**: block camera + actors together; keep image dynamic; use depth (show it or move through it).
- **Two coverage strategies**: (1) full redundancy: whole scene wide, then whole scene CU (max flexibility, max time/footage); (2) pre-editing: only opening/closing wide, bulk CU (saves time/footage, narrows options). Hitchcock pre-edited so tightly cutting sometimes meant trimming slates.

## Shooting ratio
Total footage : final running time. Varies by type/budget/style.
- Planned drama ~5:1 to 15:1. Reality TV multi-cam: up to 450 hrs for a 1-hr program.
- Drivers (drama): script length, #scenes, setups/scene, takes/setup, coverage strategy.
- **High ratio buys**: relaxed shoot, bigger risks, actors playing, no stopping between takes; in doc, catching things you'd miss.
- **High ratio costs**: rambling uncuttable shots (no clear start/end); more time/stock/storage/processing; days to view+log.
- **Film vs digital discipline**: film's cost forces edit-in-your-head, every foot counts; digital tempts endless rolling. Film-trained shoot more carefully; emulate on digital.
- Unexpected always happens (weather/flubs/tech); extra takes invariable, doc always unpredictable, budget the ratio.

## How many takes?
- **Lumet school**: rehearse thoroughly, few takes (freshest first). **Kubrick school**: many takes, groomed to perfection. Best takes = first + tenth (spontaneity vs practice); budget may cap it. Inexperienced overshoot + over-mark preferred.
- **Always ≥2 keepers** (safety vs damage/unnoticed tech faults).
- **Variation rule**: after a good take, try faster/slower/varied reading or action. Edit wants OPTIONS, not copies of one reading.
- **Busted takes**: reset fast ("back to one") without chatter; some roll into another take without reslating (helps actors, confuses edit room).
- **Circle takes**: director tells the logger which are good (circle take) + notes; digital keeps all but needs a preference record.

## Reviewing footage, dailies, pickups
- Don't replay every take on set, but DO check best takes before breaking down a camera/lighting setup.
- **Dailies**: director/DP/others evaluate as shot, ideally big screen. Raw dailies repetitive/rough; seeing potential takes experience. Some invite actors; others keep them away (self-consciousness).
- Some editors cut as shot: strong feedback (working / needs fix or reshoot).
- **Pickups & reshoots**: errors in dailies/edit need fill shots (missing cutaway/reaction), usually reduced crew. To match: stills of sets/lighting/makeup/costumes; note lenses+angles. Build reshoots/pickups/ADR into schedule + contracts.

## Directing actors (generalizes to written scenes)
### Casting
- Decisive. Huston: "The art of direction is casting. If you've casted right, you don't have to say anything." Good casting eases everything; bad casting sinks a great script.
- Fit roles AND work as ensemble. Prepare **casting breakdown** (role + short desc) + **sides** (per-character excerpts) sent before auditions; callbacks in different combinations.
- Record every audition. Try lines several ways (taking direction matters); watch listening + wordless action (acting largely nonverbal). Distrust headshots (except extras, all you have).
- Union actors = more experienced/expensive (may defer for low budget); stars unlock financing/marketing, often do a good few-day low-budget role. Open auditions = rare gems amid inexperience.
### Rehearsal
- Table read (± movement): hear dialogue, get direction ideas, build cast relationships.
- Philosophies differ: Mike Leigh uses rehearsal to shape story/dialogue; others want it fresh on set, little prep.
- Serves actors AND crew (blocking drives lighting/camera + vice versa). Don't make actors wait during lighting (stand-ins' job); marks "spiked" with tape. Once lighting/props/dolly set, flexibility drops.
### Protecting performance
- Actors vulnerable to mood disruption; respect them. Only the director gives performance notes; all else routes through director.
- Intimate/hard scenes: some want no crew eye contact while rolling; sometimes clear the set.
- Director's tone (even calling "action") sets mood. No shouting/arguments before actors; don't involve them in tech; give comfortable off-set space.
- Director wears wireless sound feed to hear dialogue as recorded.
- **Video village trap**: hiding at monitors isolates actors; a monitor crowd = too many cooks.
### Reading to camera
- Host/correspondent eye line near the lens or they look like reading. Cheapest first: cue cards with lens hole; teleprompter at lens; ear prompter/earwig replaying their own lines (needs practice; never first tried on shoot day).

## Wardrobe, makeup, set: what the camera cares about
- Avoid very bright/dark clothing. White shirts burn out at proper skin exposure (esp. daylight); use pastel/off-white.
- Avoid fine patterns (checks/stripes) → moire. Avoid shiny surfaces/jewelry (dulling spray or dry soap kills highlights; flag lights).
- Glasses: light from high or side to avoid lens kicks.
- Same rules for walls/furniture/set dressing.
- Facial shine → translucent powder (onto brush, not skin); touch up on any shine.

## Script preparation
- Narrative flow: story → treatment → screenplay in standard page format (industry expects it).
- Scripts for funders/actors: minimal camera direction/blocking: reader experiences the movie playing, not the mechanics.
- Memorable scenes use few words (looks/actions/terse exchanges beat exposition); scenes play better with less dialogue than written (pacing; audiences like connecting). **Read every line aloud before production**, ideally with actors: best time to trim dialogue + whole scenes.
- **1 script page ≈ 1 min screen time** (standard layout). Dialogue more predictable than action; verify with a stopwatch.
- Vet script for legal issues (real names/addresses, music to clear) pre-production.
### Shooting script
- Near production: specific angles, more action detail.
- Scenes numbered; scene + page numbers **locked**: grown page 18 → 18A; new scene after 20 → 20A. Revision pages dated, colored paper: 1st blue, 2nd pink, then yellow, etc.

## Previsualization ladder (simplest first)
| Tool | What | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Table read/rehearsal | Script aloud, ± movement | Trim dialogue, relationships, direction ideas |
| Floor plans | Overhead angle/blocking diagrams | Cheapest coverage planning |
| Lens sketches | Drawings of what the lens sees | Key frames of a sequence |
| Director's finder | Handheld viewer/phone app, varying focal lengths | Scouting, choosing lenses |
| Storyboards | Shot-by-shot action + camerawork drawings | FX shots, complex setups needing many aligned |
| Storyboard/previz software | Board apps; 3D camera-in-space sims | Weak drawing skill; planning set build/CGI |
| Video rough draft | Cheap video draft shot+edited pre-production (Coppola) | Testing angles/moves/dialogue/blocking; finding, with time to react, the plan should change |
- Some previz meticulously (Hitchcock claimed shooting = uneventful execution of the film in his head); others find boards limiting. Expect many board→final changes.
- A shoot isn't for fresh thinking (pausing = behind schedule, crowd waits). Decide as much as possible beforehand.

## Script breakdown & stripboard
- **Breakdown sheet** per scene lists resources: cast (principals+extras), crew, stunts, props, wardrobe, makeup/hair, vehicles, SFX+equipment, music.
- Scene length in **one-eighth-page increments** (half page = 4/8).
- **Production board (stripboard)**: one strip/scene, color-coded interior/exterior + day/night; move strips to build/revise schedule (physical or software).
- **Day-out-of-days**: every day each actor/element scheduled; reveals if rearranging films an actor's scenes back-to-back.
- Director keeps a **shot list per day**, pre-discussed with the whole team (don't assume they know), with backups for dead gear/weather/no-shows.

## Scheduling wisdom
### Prep mindset
- Shoots are chaotic (countless simultaneous uncontrollable events). Prep = CONTROLLED chaos that still delivers on schedule/budget. "Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity."
- By role: director previz's action/camera; producer hires a good team, resources ready, knows what's cuttable; DP has/knows/trusts gear, works out style + key shots with director.
- Some shoots plan to the nubs, others improvise: prepare for both.
- Gear: assemble/test full package beforehand; charge batteries night before; keep organized (in-hand in seconds); buy expendables early; hand-carry camera + delicate lenses when traveling.
### The rules
| Rule | Reason |
|---|---|
| Shoot by location, not script order | Re-setting up at one place repeatedly is inefficient |
| "Shoot out" limited-availability actors | Group all their scenes |
| Crowd wides first, CUs after | Dismiss the crowd early |
| Easy scenes first, complex later; never key scenes day one | Crew arc: bumpy start → groove → exhaustion |
| Key emotional scenes late | Actors who know each other play relationships naturally; benefit from accumulated trust |
| Exteriors relatively early; always keep an interior cover set | Bad weather won't kill the day |
| Watch the light window, not just location | Midday flat/harsh; early/late = interesting shadows; magic hour = lights+landscape; thin cloud enhances color/softens shadows (kind to CUs); haze obscures distant shots |
| Duplicate wardrobe + props | Lost prop/dirty shirt shouldn't stop production |
| Avoid company moves within a day | Moving locations burns hours |
| Meals every 6 hrs; craft service always available | Hungry crews slow down |
| Turnaround ≥12 hrs (wrap→next call) | Exhaustion causes accidents; acute on night shoots |
| Low-budget pace ~3-4 pages/12-hr day | Dialogue faster than action; many short scenes take longer than few long |
| Expect to pare the setup list | Normal to enter full, cut to essentials as time/light run out |
| Leave room for reshoots/pickups/ADR | Build into schedule + contracts |
| Backtime preproduction 3-4 months | Casting/hires/gear/permits/insurance, esp. music licenses, take months |

## On-set procedure
### Slating protocol
| # | Call | Who |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Announce take, "last looks" | AD |
| 2 | Quiet, then "sound" | AD |
| 3 | "Speed" (recorder rolling) | Sound recordist |
| 4 | "Camera" | AD |
| 5 | "Speed"/"rolling" | Camera operator |
| 6 | Read scene+take, "mark(er)", close sticks | AC |
| 7 | "Action" | Director |
| 8 | Roll until "cut" | Director |
- Slate: production co., project, director, DP, scene+take#, date; camera letter (A/B/C) if multi-cam; camera roll/card#; sound take/roll if used.
- **Scene/setup lettering**: "Scene 8" = first setup; "8A" = second angle; skip O + I (look like numbers). Alt: number slates sequentially across the whole production, bumping at each new angle.
- Slate every drama take even without sync (visual ID at head). MOS shots slated "MOS", hinged bar NOT raised.
- **Head slates** standard (fast sync). **Tail slates** (upside down, "tail slate"/"end sticks") suit delicate acted scenes + unstaged doc (slower sync). Redo → "second sticks". Slate gently (crashing clapper rattles actors; don't rush actors to start after).
- Slate on first frame = clip thumbnail in editing = free labeling.
### Logs & lined script
- **Take log** per take: date/location; card/tape/roll# (never two media same #); scene# + desc; take#; timecode start; good? + notes. In doc, log at least each day's end (what shot, which media cover what).
- Much slate info now logs as metadata; wireless timecode spares bugging the operator; editing systems import logging data.
- **Script supervisor** tracks continuity (gestures, wardrobe, weather, hair, prop state, line readings), confirms all called-for angles shot. Marks the **lined script**: vertical line down pages per setup covering them; zigzag = dialogue/action off camera in that setup. Shows coverage exists/missing at a glance.
- Also produces script notes (per-take comments, lenses, continuity) + editor's daily log in shoot order; features file a nightly production report.
- On film, AC's **camera report** lists every take per roll; circled = good (35mm usually prints only circled).

- Crew size is a balance: too small = overburdened/slow; growth multiplies (more people → cars/meals/lodging → more people), raises hourly cost, adds pressure that kills experimentation. Docs want small crews for access/low disruption.
- Daily **call sheets** (from AD): when each cast/crew reports, what's filmed, contacts, directions. Keep a production book to answer any question fast.
### Location scouting (recce)
| Area | Questions |
|---|---|
| Direction/art | Room to pull camera back? Dolly track space? Walls/furniture/art usable or must change? Bad window views? Exterior crowd control? |
| Lighting | Natural light + how it shifts over the shoot (sun apps)? How much artificial? Ceilings high enough to hide lights? Enough power/generators? |
| Camera | Special lenses (wide for small rooms)? Normal supports or handheld/Steadicam? Adjust for high/low light? |
| Sound | Quiet enough? Flight path/highway? Squeaky floors? Too reverberant? Noise change by hour? |
| Production/support | When is filming allowed, at what cost? Parking/permits? Bathrooms? Staging for gear/wardrobe/makeup? Heat? Easy to find? Internet? |
- Usually need a location release, sometimes permit + insurance bond. Building's exterior in one place + "its" interior elsewhere is routine. A studio (or quiet space + movable flats + good art direction) solves most location problems if budget allows.
- Photograph sites from many angles; a director's finder helps block shots at different focal lengths while scouting.

## Documentary styles & framework
Documentary is never a plain "document": all filming/editing = selection + POV. Pick the framework deliberately before writing/boarding any nonfiction (or fiction imitating it).
| Style | How it works | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Actuality (Edison, Lumiere) | Camera records actual events, single continuous shot | Primal form; world on film as novelty |
| Grierson hybrid | Staged events by real people (nonactors), scripted/shot like narrative; "voice of God" narration | Born when gear too heavy to chase life |
| Cinema verite/direct cinema | Lightweight sync-sound cameras react spontaneously, capture life as lived; often no narration/interviews | Unselfconscious subjects reveal how they live; self-conscious reveal via performance. Wiseman: only sync-sound scenes, no framing devices, near real time |
| Personal documentary | Filmmaker explores own life or openly acknowledges camera/role; often self-narrated | Embraces subjectivity over false objectivity |
| Magazine/correspondent | Correspondent guides: interviews, stand-ups, bites cut with B-roll | Journalism roots; short-schedule producible |
| Historical/issue | Talking heads + archival (stock footage), present-day footage, sometimes stylized reenactments (no dialogue, faces unseen) | More reenactment time/dialogue → closer to docudrama |
| Reality TV | People in preplanned situations/competitions; unscripted dialogue in a designed setup | Cut into episode format |
- **Script or no script**: historical/issue films start with research → script/treatment; preinterviews establish roughly what people say; footage illustrates preset ideas. Opposite pole: shooting IS the research: follow threads/characters, arc emerges in shooting + long edit. Some stories have an obvious arc (an election). The risky observed-life approach yields characters who change/are born/die: unmatched depth.
- **Framework questions up front**: how does the audience learn the subject: events unfolding verite? interviews over other footage? experts? narration, and if so a disembodied voice or someone on screen (subject/filmmaker/correspondent)?
### Filming real life
- More trust = more comfort on camera. Spend time camera-off; discuss the kind of film, where it shows, what's okay to shoot. Get releases (early or later) but don't grant edit approval: you make the movie. Exhibition can seriously affect subjects' lives; weigh it while shooting/editing.
- Small crew, few lights (relaxed/low-key). Camera on shoulder most of the time (no shooting/waiting divide); tally light off; quiet slates, tail slates, or none. Not sneaky: subtle, fluid transition.
- Wireless mics free subjects to roam (no boom); show them the mute for privacy.
- Being filmed gets boring fast; novelty wears off: what you want. From fly-on-the-wall silence to continuous conversation: pick one, be consistent.

## Documentary principles that generalize
- Verite operators must **think like director AND editor** while reacting spontaneously. Fight the urge to fixate on the action/talker; audience also needs context (wide) + reactions. Think of the whole sequence, keep asking if coverage is enough, provide options + possible beginnings/endings.
- **Give every shot/move a shape**: hold a static frame, move, glide to a stop, hold. Aimless constant movement = uncuttable.
- **Shoot so time condenses**: a 2-hr dinner plays as 2 min. A locked two-shot is uncuttable; get varied angles (two-shots + CUs) + lots of the LISTENER. An OTS behind the talker hides moving lips, carries any audio stretch; same for phone calls (phone blocks lips) + musicians (neutral shots, no visible finger positions).
- Cutaways are often essential to making a doc scene work at all.
- **Interview craft**: decide if the interviewer's voice is heard. If not, subjects fold the question into the answer ("I was born in London", not "London"), speak full sentences, never "as I said before" or "you". Relaxed tone; explain you'll nod silently. Listen for editability; break long run-ons, ask succinctly or in short bits. First answer freshest; on re-ask, change focal length so parts of two answers cut together.
- **Interview camera language**: alternate subjects facing screen left/right; opposing opinions get opposing screen directions. A setting can characterize a speaker (kitchen/activity/walking/driving); neutral/limbo backdrop = consistency across many subjects but dull; green screen opens background but can feel artificial. Some shoot static, changing focal length only BETWEEN shots (never mid-zoom); a slow zoom during emotion works if paced to the speech phrases. A dolly on curved track keeps movement at constant distance. Multiple cameras ease condensing without jump cuts + avoid the faked-later reaction shot (mocked in Broadcast News).
- Leave room at frame bottom if lower-thirds will identify speakers.
- If only interview audio is used (VO narration by the subject), record audio-only or point the camera away to put people at ease.
- Same discipline for any "found footage"/mockumentary you write: the fiction must obey real documentary physics + grammar to be credible.
